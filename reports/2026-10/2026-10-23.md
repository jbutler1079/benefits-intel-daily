### Final Transparency in Coverage rule cuts allowed-amount claims threshold from 20 to 11 and moves machine-readable files from monthly to quarterly — effective Dec 7, 2026
- **Source:** U.S. Treasury/IRS, DOL/EBSA, HHS/CMS, "Transparency in Coverage" final rule (CMS-9882-F; TD 10058; RIN 1210-AC30), 91 Fed. Reg. 63748, Oct 6, 2026
- **Methodology/sample:** Final regulation text (PDF read and text-extracted from govinfo.gov); effective date and cadence/threshold changes verified in the text. The Departments' regulatory impact analysis estimates about 1,300,000 hours of ongoing annual burden (about $60.45M) for the phone-based cost-estimate requirement across 1,508 issuers and TPAs under its high-impact scenario.
- **The number:** Out-of-network allowed-amount reporting threshold lowered from 20 claims to 11; file reporting cadence drops from monthly to quarterly; pricing info available via online tool/paper must also be available by phone; effective Dec 7, 2026. (Phase-in dates for individual provisions, e.g. Jan 2027 / Mar 2027 / Sep 2027, come from secondary summaries and were not verified; check the rule text before citing. A secondary claim of a "ghost rate ban" was not found in the rule text searched.)
- **Why it matters:** More out-of-network allowed-amount data will become visible, but quarterly files and the 11-claim floor still leave thin-volume, high-cost services suppressed (commenters said 11 "does not go far enough"). Employers in self-funded plans still depend on their TPA for claims-level data, so contractual data access matters more than public files. *Ties to TX-APCD data gaps and unbundled/direct-contracting positioning: public files remain a partial substitute for plan-level claims data. Flag: moderate-high.*
- **Social angle:** LinkedIn take: "New federal price-transparency rule: files get smaller and less frequent, and out-of-network data gets slightly deeper. Does your TPA contract give you your own claims data?" Simple before/after graphic (20→11 claims; monthly→quarterly).

### Private equity owns just 3.5% of U.S. short-term acute-care hospitals (2.5% of beds, 1.8% of discharges) — and about two-thirds of PE-acquired hospitals change owners again within 5 years
- **Source:** McDevitt, Naslund et al. (WashU Olin, Duke, UC Berkeley, UIUC, Indiana U), "Three Facts About Private Equity in the U.S. Hospital Sector," *Health Affairs Scholar* vol. 4, issue 9; WashU release Oct 2026
- **Methodology/sample:** Six commercial deal databases (PitchBook, Irving Levin, Preqin, Capital IQ, FactSet, SDC Platinum); 556 short-term acute-care hospitals acquired in 139 PE deals, 2000–2024. Funders include the American Investment Council (a PE trade group); the article states funders did not review the work. Does not assess PE impact on prices or quality, and excludes physician practices and specialty facilities.
- **The number:** 3.5% of hospitals / 2.5% of beds / 1.8% of discharges (2024); ~2/3 of acquired hospitals re-sold within 5 years; only 1 stayed with its original PE owner 15 years; in 2023 over 2/3 of Americans lived in referral regions with no PE-owned hospital.
- **Why it matters:** Counterintuitive for an employer audience: hospital price pressure is mostly not a PE story at the hospital level, so negotiating strategy should focus on nonprofit and system market power. Pair with the separate JAMA Health Forum finding (Apr 2026, via AHIP) that hospital-affiliated primary care practices charge ~10.7% more than independent practices and PE-affiliated ones ~7.8% more (secondary source; not verified against the paper). *No specific direct-contracting/TX-APCD tie; context stat.*
- **Social angle:** Single-stat card: "PE owns 3.5% of US hospitals — so who's setting your prices?" Caveat the industry funding in the caption.

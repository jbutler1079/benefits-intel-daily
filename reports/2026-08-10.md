### A peer-reviewed reference-based pricing program paid hospitals 140% of Medicare — while the commercial market average sits at 254%, and it still saved employers $417 million
- **Source:** Roslyn C. Murray & Christopher M. Whaley (Brown University), "Commercial Prices Relative To Medicare Were Lower Under An Employer-Based Reference Pricing Program," *Health Affairs Scholar*, Vol. 4, Issue 7, published July 2026.
- **Methodology/sample:** Analyzed 2020–2022 medical claims for a large population of privately insured individuals enrolled in an employer-sponsored reference-based pricing (RBP) program, comparing prices paid to hospitals and other facilities nationwide against prices paid by other commercial payers in Round 5.1 of the RAND Employer Hospital Price Transparency Study.
- **The number:** The RBP program paid hospitals ~140% of Medicare rates on average — 118 percentage points lower than the broader commercial market's ~254%-of-Medicare average for inpatient services, and 162 points lower for outpatient services. Relative to what other commercial payers paid for the same care, the RBP program's employers saved an estimated $417 million, or 56.5% of what they would have spent paying standard commercial rates.
- **Why it matters:** Flag prominently — this is a direct, peer-reviewed, dollar-denominated answer to "does unbundling from standard commercial network pricing actually save money," using real claims data rather than a vendor-marketed projection. A 56.5% relative saving is a concrete anchor stat for any pitch built around reference-based pricing or direct-contracting as an alternative to traditional TPA network deals — and it lands the same week the CLAUDE.md-tracked "Fair Pay, Fair Play TPA" policy proposal (below) argues the standard TPA model itself needs disrupting.
- **Social angle:** Flag prominently — single striking-number graphic: "254% of Medicare (standard commercial) vs. 140% of Medicare (reference-based pricing)" with the $417M/56.5% savings figure as the caption. Strong candidate for a LinkedIn post targeted at CFOs/benefits leaders evaluating RBP or direct-contracting for 2027 renewal.

### A new Health Affairs proposal argues Congress should let self-funded employers bypass the standard insurer-as-TPA model entirely
- **Source:** Christine H. Monahan, Kennah Watts, Karen Handorf & Kevin W. Lucia, "The Fair Pay, Fair Play TPA: A Proposal To Disrupt The Market For Health Plan Administration," *Health Affairs Forefront*, published August 7, 2026. (Accessed via RamaOnHealthcare's summary; the primary Health Affairs Forefront piece was not independently retrievable through available tools today, so figures below are limited to what's corroborated across secondary coverage.)
- **Methodology/sample:** Policy proposal (not an empirical study) from health policy researchers affiliated with Georgetown's Center on Health Insurance Reforms, addressing how the ~165.6 million non-elderly Americans with employer-sponsored coverage — the majority in self-funded plans — are administered today: employers bear the financial risk but typically hire a large insurance company as TPA to build networks, negotiate rates, and manage claims.
- **The number:** 165.6 million non-elderly Americans covered through employer-sponsored plans, most self-funded and administered through the standard insurer-as-TPA arrangement the proposal specifically targets for disruption.
- **Why it matters:** Flag prominently — this is a live, three-days-old policy argument for exactly the unbundled/direct-contracting alternative this project tracks, coming from health policy researchers rather than a vendor. It's directional rather than data-heavy (no specific fee cap or mechanism confirmed independently today), but it signals the "let employers unbundle TPA services" argument is gaining a policy platform, not just a vendor sales pitch — worth revisiting once the full text or a more detailed writeup is accessible.
- **Social angle:** Pair with the RBP finding above as a two-part thread: "The data says unbundling saves money (56.5%, peer-reviewed) — and policy researchers are now arguing Congress should make it easier to do." Hold the full citation/mechanism details for a follow-up post once the primary source is confirmed.

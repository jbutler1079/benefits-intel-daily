### At rural critical access hospitals, more commercial business was tied to *lower* operating margins, not higher. Commercial share rose from 39.9% to 44.8% (2011–2023) while median operating margin fell from 9.7% to 4.4%
- **Source:** Jia, Li, Cai, Enumah (University of Rochester Medical Center), "Payer mix shifts and profitability at critical access hospitals, 2011 to 2023," *Health Affairs Scholar* 4(4), qxag083, published online Apr 7, 2026 (open access).
- **Methodology/sample:** 15,819 hospital-year observations from 1,384 critical access hospitals (CAHs), 2011–2023, using the NASHP Hospital Cost Tool (built on Medicare Cost Reports) linked to the AHA Annual Survey. Linear mixed-effects models with hospital random intercepts and year and state fixed effects; commercial mix is the reference group; top and bottom 1% of margins excluded. The authors call it observational and hypothesis-generating, with no adjustment for multiple comparisons. I read the full paper on the journal site.
- **The number:** Each 1-point shift of payer mix from commercial to Medicare was associated with a **+0.10-point higher operating margin** (95% CI 0.05–0.15). The same shift to Medicaid was associated with +0.09 (0.04–0.14). Trends, 2011 to 2023: commercial mix 39.9% → 44.8%; Medicare mix 40.4% → 34.6%; median operating margin 9.7% → 4.4%. Medicaid-specific margins stayed around −36% to −38%. Caveat: "Medicare mix" includes Medicare Advantage, but the Medicare margin measures traditional Medicare only (the NASHP data has no MA margins for CAHs).
- **Why it matters:** CAHs are paid by traditional Medicare on a cost basis, so for them Medicare is not the money-loser that hospital lobbying implies. The "we charge commercial plans more to cover Medicare losses" argument is weakest at exactly the rural hospitals where employers have the least negotiating leverage. Rural employers should ask for the hospital's own cost-report data (NASHP breakeven) before accepting a cost-shift justification. One caution: commercial *payer-specific* margins rose with Medicare and Medicaid share, so a hospital with a thin commercial book may push harder on commercial rates. That makes the case for anchoring prices to cost, not to the hospital's payer mix.
- **Social angle:** LinkedIn take plus a two-line chart (commercial mix up, median margin down, 2011–2023): "At rural critical access hospitals, Medicare pays on cost. So the cost-shift story doesn't hold the way you've heard it. Ask for the cost report." Note in the caption that the study shows associations, not causes.
- **Flags:** Ties to direct-contracting and cost-plus/reference-based positioning (cost-report–anchored pricing for rural hospitals), and directly to HPHP's rural High Plains footprint. TX-APCD angle: the study uses Medicare Cost Reports precisely because commercial-paid data at rural hospitals is scarce. TX-APCD excludes large self-funded employers, so it can't fill that gap for the employers HPHP serves.

### Nonprofit hospitals in the neediest communities set the same free-care income cutoffs as hospitals in better-off areas; 168 hospitals cap free care at 100% of poverty (~$33,000 for a family of four)
- **Source:** Tatiane Santos et al. (Tulane University), "Not-For-Profit Hospitals' Patient Financial Assistance And Alignment With Community Need: A National Study," *Health Affairs*, Nov 2, 2026 (DOI 10.1377/hlthaff.2026.00268).
- **Methodology/sample:** About 10 years (2013–2022) of IRS Form 990 Schedule H data for more than 1,600 nonprofit hospitals, linked to community-need measures. The journal page was blocked (403), so I read the Tulane press release (EurekAlert, Nov 2026), not the paper. Figures should be checked against the article before posting.
- **The number:** High-need and low-need hospitals had "essentially the same" income eligibility thresholds. Of the hospitals spending less than the benchmark on charity care, 461 limit free care to ≤200% FPL (~$66,000 for a family of four) and 168 to ≤100% FPL (~$33,000), 34 of them in high-need areas. The typical hospital spends ~1% of its operating budget (~$142M) on charity care. In high-need areas, more generous thresholds were associated with ~$383,000 more charity care per hospital per year. Raising the 168 hospitals to 200% FPL was estimated at ~$710,000 more per hospital per year. Nonprofit hospital tax exemptions were valued at $37.4B in 2021.
- **Why it matters:** Employees in high-deductible plans are often the "insured but can't pay" patients that charity-care policies leave out. When a nonprofit system says it can't cut commercial rates because of its community mission, its Form 990 shows how much charity care it actually gives (~1% of budget) and who qualifies. That is useful in direct-contracting talks and in employee-advocacy programs that steer members toward hospital financial assistance.
- **Social angle:** Thread: "$37.4B in tax exemptions. ~1% of budget on charity care. 168 hospitals cap free care at $33K for a family of four." End with how employers can look up a local system's Schedule H. Chart: count of hospitals by FPL threshold (100% vs 200%).
- **Flags:** Indirect tie to direct-contracting positioning (Form 990 as a negotiating input; source #27). TX-APCD angle: none.

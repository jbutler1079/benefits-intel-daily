# Daily Research — 2026-11-13

**One finding met the bar today: a new Health Affairs study from the November 2026 print issue. Hospitals and insurers get cheaper to run as they grow, but purchasers' spending does not fall with scale. The same filings show ASO administration costs about half as much per dollar as fully insured commercial coverage. The PBGH virtual primary care review (Nov 12) is listed as a watch item because the public release has too little data to cite.**

### Big hospitals and insurers get economies of scale (each 1% of revenue growth adds only 0.92–0.94% to admin cost), but purchasers' spending still rises in step with volume. ASO admin runs 4.8% of spending, compared with 9.3% for fully insured commercial
- **Source:** Buxbaum JD, Ryan AM, "Health Care Purchasers Do Not Consistently Benefit From Lower Administrative Costs Of Large Hospitals And Insurers," *Health Affairs* 45(11):1200–1209, November 2026 (online Nov 2, 2026; DOI 10.1377/hlthaff.2026.00279).
- **Methodology/sample:** The authors used 2024 regulatory filings for hospitals and insurers. The abstract does not name the filings; likely Medicare cost reports and insurer financial/MLR filings, but this is unverified. For each group, they estimated how volume, revenue and administrative spending relate: hospitals measured by adjusted net patient revenue and discharges, insurers by covered lives and premiums, split by market segment. The study is cross-sectional and observational. I read the full abstract through the OpenAlex index of the DOI. The full text is paywalled, so I couldn't check the sample counts or the segment-level regression tables.
- **The number:**
  - Median hospital **administrative cost = 25.5% of adjusted net patient revenue**.
  - Insurer administrative spending by segment: **ASO (self-funded administration) 4.8%**, **fully insured commercial 9.3%**, Medicaid managed care 7.3%, **Medicare Advantage 10.1%** (the highest).
  - Economies of scale: **each 1.00% increase in revenue was associated with a 0.92–0.94% increase in admin cost**, for both hospitals and insurers.
  - Even so, "purchasers of hospital care and insurance coverage did not demonstrably benefit." Purchaser spending **rose roughly in proportion to hospital discharges and covered lives**. The savings from scale stayed with the hospitals and insurers.
- **Why it matters:** This is a peer-reviewed answer to the claim that merged health systems and big carriers are more efficient. They are, but employers don't get the savings. For the employer, the useful split is ASO admin at 4.8% vs. fully insured commercial at 9.3%. Self-funding is about half as costly to administer per dollar before any network or pricing strategy, and it is the arrangement where the employer can see and contract around the scale gains. The authors also call for mechanisms that make providers and insurers share scale savings, and direct contracts with price terms are one such mechanism a plan sponsor can use.
- **Social angle:** LinkedIn take: "Health systems keep saying mergers create efficiency. A new Health Affairs study agrees, then shows employers got none of it." Pair it with a simple bar chart of insurer admin % by segment (ASO 4.8 / Medicaid MCO 7.3 / fully insured commercial 9.3 / MA 10.1) with the ASO bar highlighted. A carousel slide on the 25.5% hospital admin share works as a stand-alone stat card.
- **Flags:** **Unbundled/direct-contracting positioning: strong tie.** It quantifies the ASO admin advantage and shows that consolidation savings don't reach purchasers unless someone contracts for them. **TX-APCD: no direct tie.** Employers can run the same hospital-admin-share check for Texas hospitals they negotiate with, using CMS cost reports (source #4).

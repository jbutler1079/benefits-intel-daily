1. **Dialysis: adding a second, third or fourth big chain doesn't lower prices. Only an independent clinic does.** Markets where one large chain operates pay **$495 more per commercial dialysis session (~$1,334 vs. ~$839)** than markets with no large chain. Markets with two to four large chains pay no less than monopoly markets (Xia, McDevitt et al., *JAMA Health Forum*, June 2025).
2. **Hospital-owned primary care costs 10.7% more for the same office visit.** Nearly half (**47.9%**) of primary care physicians are now hospital-affiliated, up from 25.2% in 2009. Paying them independent rates would have cut 2022 commercial office-visit spending at those practices by **$1.5B–$1.8B** (Singh, Whaley et al., *JAMA Health Forum*, Jan. 2025).
3. **Employer plans pay 2x Medicare for a ground ambulance ride, and over 3x in four states.** The No Surprises Act doesn't cover ground ambulances, and state surprise-billing laws don't reach self-funded plans (HCCI data brief, Dec. 2024).

### In dialysis, a single large chain means $495 more per commercial session (~59% more). Markets with 2, 3 or 4 large chains are no cheaper. Only markets with no large chain, or with an independent clinic, pay less
- **Source:** Xuyang Xia, Wanrong Deng, Paul J. Eliason, Riley J. League, Ryan C. McDevitt, James W. Roberts and Heather Wong, "Financial Ties, Market Structure, Commercial Prices, and Medical Director Compensation in Dialysis," *JAMA Health Forum* 2025;6(6):e252659, June 18, 2025 (conflict-of-interest correction Nov. 7, 2025). [Article](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama-health-forum/fullarticle/2835489)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - This is a retrospective economic evaluation covering 2005–2019. It links FOIA'd CMS ownership (PECOS) and ESRD medical-director records with cost reports, USRDS Medicare claims, and **HCCI commercial claims from employer-sponsored plans (2012–2020)**.
  - The panel has **99,496 facility-years** and **~2.1M price observations**. The outcome is the commercial allowed amount per outpatient hemodialysis session, with the top 1% trimmed.
  - Markets are hospital service areas, classified by how many of the five largest chains are present: DaVita, Fresenius, US Renal Care, American Renal Associates and Dialysis Clinic Inc. The regressions use month-year fixed effects and facility-count fixed effects.
  - The results are associations, not causal estimates.
- **The number:**
  - **Price gap:** markets with one large chain averaged **$1,333.90 per session**. Markets with no large chain paid **$495.08 less** (95% CI roughly $371–$619), or **37.1% less**.
  - **Price trend:** descriptively, no-chain markets went from **$929 to $827** per session over the sample. One-chain markets went from **$1,293 to $1,363**.
  - **Rival chains don't help:** two, three or four large chains showed **no meaningful price difference** from one chain (e.g., +$31 for two chains, CI includes zero).
  - **Independents do help:** an independent facility in the market was associated with **$112 lower** prices (~8%).
  - **Consolidation:** DaVita and Fresenius grew from **59.1% to 77.1%** of facilities, while independents fell from 20.4% to 10.6%. By 2019, **32.5%** of Americans lived in an area served only by DaVita or Fresenius, including **99% of Minnesotans**.
  - **Physician ties:** physician-owned facilities rose from **11.4% to 29.1%**, and 81% of those are joint ventures with chains. In one-chain markets, medical directors were paid **$565 more per patient**.
  - **Medicare comparison:** Medicare prices did **not** differ across market types, and neither did standardized one-year mortality. The higher commercial price buys no measurable quality.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Commercial plans pay the monopoly premium alone. For an employer, one dialysis patient (often ~150 sessions a year) at a ~$500-per-session gap is roughly **$75K a year**, and stop-loss renewals price that in.
  - The usual hope that competition will fix this doesn't hold here. Adding a second national chain didn't move price, which points to coordinated pricing or reliance on physician referral networks rather than price competition.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** this is a strong case for carving dialysis out to a contracted rate, such as a percent-of-Medicare or an independent-clinic contract, rather than accepting the network's rate. A carrier network has little reason to steer away from the chains.
  - **Plan-design context:** in *Marietta Memorial Hospital v. DaVita* (2022), the Supreme Court held that a plan paying dialysis at uniform, lower rates didn't violate the Medicare Secondary Payer Act. Have ERISA counsel review any dialysis carve-out.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Stat card:** "One big dialysis chain in town: $1,334 a session. No big chain: $839. Same Medicare price. Same mortality."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "In dialysis, competition between big chains didn't lower prices. 2, 3, even 4 chains priced like a monopoly. The only thing that moved price was an independent clinic. If your plan just pays whatever the network rate is, you're paying the monopoly price."
  - **Chart:** bar chart of commercial price by number of large chains (0, 1, 2, 3, 4), where the only drop is at zero.

### The same primary care visit costs 10.7% more at a hospital-owned practice and 7.8% more at a PE-owned one. Hospital affiliation nearly doubled, to 47.9% of PCPs, and Texas ranks #2 for PE penetration
- **Source:** Yashaswini Singh, Nandita Radhakrishnan, Loren Adler, Christopher Whaley et al., "Growth of Private Equity and Hospital Consolidation in Primary Care and Price Implications," *JAMA Health Forum* 2025;6(1):e244935, Jan. 17, 2025. [Article](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama-health-forum/fullarticle/2829224)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - Affiliation trends come from IQVIA SK&A (2009–2019) and PitchBook, covering **198,097 PCPs**.
  - Prices come from **2022 Transparency in Coverage negotiated rates** (via Clarify Health): **226M+ negotiated prices** for **115,811 PCPs** across Aetna, BCBS, Cigna and UnitedHealthcare.
  - Only E/M office visits are included (99202–99205 and 99211–99215).
  - The regression is volume-weighted, with CPT and insurer-state fixed effects. It is cross-sectional, so it doesn't establish causation.
- **The number:**
  - **Affiliation trend:** hospital-affiliated PCPs rose from **25.2% (2009) to 47.9% (2022)**. PE-affiliated PCPs rose from under 0.1% before 2013 to **1.5%**.
  - **States:** **Texas is #2 for PE share (3.4%)**, behind Florida (6.3%). North Dakota (86.2%) and Wisconsin (83.2%) have the highest hospital-affiliation shares.
  - **Adjusted price premium vs. independent PCPs:** hospital-affiliated practices charge **+$14.91 (+10.7%)** and PE-affiliated practices **+$9.56 (+7.8%)**. Both are significant at p < .001, and the two premiums differ significantly from each other (p = .006).
  - **Unadjusted mean new-patient visit:** **$180 (hospital) vs. $155 (PE) vs. $147 (independent)**.
  - **Premium by visit level:** the gap grows with visit complexity. It is **+$110** on a 99204, **+$183** on a 99205, and **+$116** on a 99215.
  - **Spending:** 2022 commercial office-visit spending at hospital-affiliated PCPs was **$10.9B**. At independent rates it would have been **15–18% lower ($1.5B–$1.8B)**.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Primary care is the entry point to the network. A hospital-owned PCP costs more per visit, and on Nov. 23 we reported that hospital-acquired practices also bill more Level-5 visits and refer imaging and labs to higher-priced facility settings.
  - The price premium is the smaller part of the cost. Who controls the referral matters more.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** contracting directly with independent primary care, whether DPC or advanced primary care, avoids both the visit premium and the in-system referral pattern.
  - **TX angle:** Texas has the second-highest PE share of PCPs in the country. Pair this with the Nov. 1 finding on Texas PE hospital ownership for a "who owns your doctor in Texas" post.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Carousel:** slide 1, "Half of primary care doctors now work for a hospital (up from 1 in 4)." Slide 2, "Same visit: $180 vs. $147." Slide 3, "Your plan pays $1.5B–$1.8B a year for the logo on the door."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "The most expensive part of a hospital-owned PCP isn't the 10.7% visit premium. It's where they send you next."

### Employer plans pay 2.0x Medicare for a ground ambulance ride ($718 vs. $365 base rate), and more than 3x in Minnesota, California, Illinois and New Jersey. Federal surprise-billing law doesn't cover ground ambulances, and state laws don't reach self-funded plans
- **Source:** John Hargraves, Kelsey Burke, Jessica Chang and Katie Martin, "Commercial Prices for Ground Ambulance Are Double Medicare Rates," Health Care Cost Institute data brief, Dec. 16, 2024. [Brief](https://healthcostinstitute.org/all-hcci-reports/commercial-prices-for-ground-ambulance-are-double-medicare-rates/)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - The data are HCCI employer-sponsored insurance (ESI) claims compared with Medicare ground-ambulance reimbursement, 2016–2022.
  - Prices are standardized to a **5-mile one-way trip**, the median ESI trip, combining the base rate and mileage. Ground ambulances make up 98% of ambulance trips.
  - State ratios are reported for 2022.
- **The number:**
  - **Base rate (2022):** median ESI **$718** vs. Medicare **$365** (**2.0x**), up from **1.8x** in 2016 ($574 vs. $315).
  - **Mileage:** **$17/mile vs. $8 (2.1x)**.
  - **Trend:** the ESI-to-Medicare ratio rose **9%** from 2016 to 2022.
  - **States above 3x Medicare:** Minnesota, California, Illinois and New Jersey. Ohio, Kentucky, Maryland and Maine are near 1.0x. Colorado requires state-regulated plans to pay out-of-network trips at **325% of Medicare**, which sets a high floor.
  - **Out-of-network context (earlier HCCI work):** **35%** of ESI ground-ambulance trips were out-of-network in 2021, down from 43% in 2012.
- **Why it matters:**
  - The **No Surprises Act excludes ground ambulances**, and state ground-ambulance balance-billing laws generally can't reach **self-funded ERISA plans** because of preemption. Self-funded members are the most exposed group.
  - That leaves the plan with two bad options: pay billed charges, or leave the member with a balance bill.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** self-funded plans can write their own ground-ambulance payment rule (e.g., a percent of Medicare plus member balance-bill protection). They can also contract directly with municipal or private EMS providers. A carrier's fully insured product just follows state law.
  - **Caveat:** these are 2022 prices, and Texas isn't broken out. Texas state law (SB 2476, 2023) addressed ground-ambulance balance billing for *state-regulated* plans, which doesn't help self-funded members. Verify the current statute before citing.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Map:** ESI-to-Medicare ratio by state, with the four >3x states highlighted.
  - **LinkedIn take:** "Your employees are protected from surprise ER bills, but not from the ambulance ride that got them there. If you're self-funded, the state law probably doesn't apply either. That gap is yours to close in the plan document."

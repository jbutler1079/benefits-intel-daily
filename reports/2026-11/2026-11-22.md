# Daily Research — 2026-11-22

**Three findings met the bar today. All come from the Massachusetts state releases at the Nov. 10, 2026 Health Care Cost Trends Hearing, which this project had not yet covered.** Two use the state's All-Payer Claims Database (the Health Policy Commission's research deck), and one is the Attorney General's 2026 cost-trends examination. Massachusetts is the clearest comparison state for what an APCD can show employers, and Texas's cannot (see TX-APCD flags).

1. **Colonoscopy with polyp removal ranged from $601 to $3,679** in Massachusetts commercial claims. Hospital outpatient departments averaged **64% more than ASCs and 167% more than physician offices**, and volume is shifting toward hospitals.

Insurers paid another **$489M** in "supplemental" payments on top of $1.6B in fee-for-service primary care, and organizations passed only **0–48%** of that money to primary care.
3. **Ten drugs drove more than 80% of commercial net pharmacy spending growth** in Massachusetts from 2020 to 2024. Skyrizi alone was 21% and Wegovy 17%; four GLP-1s together were 32%.

### A colonoscopy with polyp removal cost $601 to $3,679 (6.1x) in the same state. Hospital outpatient departments averaged 64% more than ASCs and 167% more than physician offices, and volume is moving toward the hospital
- **Source:** Massachusetts Health Policy Commission (HPC), *Data Insights on Health Care Cost Drivers and Dynamics*, research presentation by Executive Director David Seltz at the 2026 Health Care Cost Trends Hearing, Nov. 10, 2026 (slide 34).
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - HPC analysis of the CHIA Massachusetts All-Payer Claims Database (V2024, 2022–2024 commercial claims).
  - Service: CPT 45385 (flexible colonoscopy with snare removal of polyps or lesions).
  - Prices are built at the encounter level (same person, date and procedure code), so facility and professional claims billed the same day are combined.
- **The number:**
  - 2024 prices ranged from **$601 to $3,679**, a 6.1x spread.
  - The average HOPD price was **64% higher than at an ASC** and **167% higher than in an office setting**.
  - HPC also reports that volume is **shifting toward the HOPD setting**.
  - **Context from the same deck:** Massachusetts commercial spending growth accelerated in every care setting from 2022 to 2024 compared with 2019–2022. HOPDs made up **89%** of spending in the HOPD/ED/ASC category in 2024. HPC lists site-of-care shift, coding intensity and service mix as price-related drivers on top of unit-price growth.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Colonoscopy is a high-volume, shoppable procedure that is often preventive. For a self-funded plan, steering it from HOPD to ASC or office saves real money on every case, with no change in the care itself.
  - The volume shift toward hospitals means doing nothing costs more each year, as hospital-owned GI practices bill under the higher-priced setting.
  - **Ties to direct-contracting positioning:** this case suits a bundled, direct-contracted ASC price with $0 member cost-sharing. Site-neutral contracting written into the plan document addresses the shift directly.
  - **TX-APCD gap:** HPC can produce this chart because the Massachusetts APCD carries commercial paid amounts across settings (since *Gobeille*, self-insured ERISA plans submit voluntarily there too, so coverage is incomplete in both states). TX-APCD excludes self-funded ERISA plans, so a Texas employer can't get a comparable statewide view of what plans like theirs pay by setting. **Flag as a TX-APCD data gap.**
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** three bars (Office, ASC, HOPD) for the same CPT code, captioned "Same colonoscopy. Same polyp removal. 167% more in the hospital."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "The cheapest colonoscopy in Massachusetts was $601. The most expensive was $3,679. Your plan document decides which one your employees get."

### Same primary care visit, different price: commercial rates ran from 127% to 244% of Medicare across 10 Massachusetts provider organizations. Insurers paid another $489M in "supplemental" payments, and some groups passed none of it to primary care
- **Source:** Massachusetts Attorney General's Office (AG Andrea Joy Campbell), *2026 Examination of Health Care Cost Trends and Cost Drivers: Commercial Supplemental Payments and Primary Care Payment Disparities*, released Nov. 10, 2026. Figures here come from the AG press release and local coverage of the report (This Week in Worcester, Nov. 13). The report PDF on mass.gov could not be retrieved today, so check exact figures against it before posting.
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - 2024 commercial primary care payments for a sample of Massachusetts payers and 10 provider organizations, collected under the AG's statutory cost-trends authority.
  - Claims-based rates are compared with Medicare's 2024 Massachusetts fee schedule.
  - Supplemental payments (non-claims payments such as care-management, quality and infrastructure dollars) were collected and attributed to primary care.
- **The number:**
  - **Primary care rates relative to Medicare:**

    | Provider organization | Rate (% of Medicare) |
    |---|---|
    | Mass General Brigham | 244% |
    | UMass Memorial | 222% |
    | South Shore | 207% |
    | Tufts Medicine | 188% |
    | Optum (Atrius, Reliant) | 184% |
    | Beth Israel Lahey | 184% |
    | Cape Cod Healthcare | 180% |
    | Southcoast | 171% |
    | Signature | 169% |
    | Community Care Cooperative (C3, community health centers) | 127% |

  - **Spread:** the highest rate is **1.9x the lowest** for the same services.
  - **Supplemental payments:** **$489M** in 2024, about **30% on top of $1.6B** in core fee-for-service primary care payments. The largest recipients were Optum ($115.1M), Tufts ($76.2M), MGB ($71.8M), Steward ($65.2M) and BILH ($54.7M).
  - **Pass-through:** one organization passed **0%** of its supplemental payments to primary care. The other nine estimated **22–48%**.
  - The AG found that adding supplemental payments **increases** variation in payment levels; it doesn't offset it.
  - **Self-funded share:** at one large commercial insurer, **self-insured plans accounted for 64% of primary care visits**.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Primary care is usually treated as the cheap, good part of the plan. But the dominant academic systems charge nearly twice what community health centers get for the same codes, and most of the money labeled "for primary care" flows to the system, not to primary care.
  - Self-insured employers fund most of this (64% of visits at one carrier) and rarely see the supplemental payment layer. It sits outside claims data in carrier–system contracts.
  - **Ties to direct-contracting positioning:** direct primary care or a direct-contracted primary care fee at a stated multiple of Medicare skips both the 1.9x rate spread and the opaque supplemental layer. It also supports the CAA fiduciary argument: employers should ask carriers to disclose non-claims payments that their claims fund.
  - **TX-APCD gap:** non-claims and supplemental payments appear in no claims database, Texas's included. Massachusetts only knows this because the AG can compel disclosure. **Flag as a TX-APCD data gap**, and as a contract-language issue to raise with any ASO carrier.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** ranked horizontal bar chart of the 10 organizations by percent of Medicare, with C3 at 127% highlighted as the community health center benchmark.
  - **Thread:** "Your plan pays for primary care twice: once per claim, and once through 'supplemental payments' you never see. In Massachusetts that second check was $489M, and up to 100% of it never reached a primary care doctor."

### Ten drugs drove more than 80% of commercial net pharmacy spending growth in Massachusetts from 2020 to 2024. Skyrizi alone was 21% and Wegovy 17%; four GLP-1s together were 32%
- **Source:** Massachusetts Health Policy Commission, *Data Insights on Health Care Cost Drivers and Dynamics*, 2026 Cost Trends Hearing, Nov. 10, 2026 (slide 35).
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - HPC analysis of CHIA APCD v2024 commercial retail pharmacy claims (2020–2024).
  - Net of rebates, using the SSR Health US Brand Rx Net Pricing Tool and CHIA's prescription drug rebate data.
  - Extrapolated to the full Massachusetts commercial population with CHIA enrollment data.
- **The number:**
  - **Top 10 drugs by net spending growth, 2020→2024, with each one's share of total net pharmacy spending growth:**

    | Drug | Net spending 2020 → 2024 | Change | Share of growth |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Skyrizi | $37.8M → $411.5M | +$373.7M | 21% |
    | Wegovy | $0 → $292.4M | +$292.4M | 17% |
    | Dupixent | $73.7M → $262.9M | +$189.2M | 11% |
    | Zepbound | $0 → $143.4M | +$143.4M | 8% |
    | Rinvoq | $9.7M → $121.3M | +$111.6M | 6% |
    | Stelara | $182.0M → $275.4M | +$93.4M | 5% |
    | Kesimpta | $0.3M → $80.0M | +$79.6M | 5% |
    | Mounjaro | $0 → $75.0M | +$75.0M | 4% |
    | Ozempic | $12.8M → $73.2M | +$60.3M | 3% |
    | Verzenio | $11.7M → $63.4M | +$51.6M | 3% |

  - **Immunology:** the four immunosuppressants (Skyrizi, Dupixent, Rinvoq, Stelara) account for about **43%** of growth.
  - **GLP-1s:** the four GLP-1s (Wegovy, Zepbound, Mounjaro, Ozempic) account for about **32%**.
  - **Pre-filed testimony:** BCBS Massachusetts named medical-pharmacy and retail-pharmacy spending as two of its three most concerning trend drivers. A filer also noted that **80%** of prescription claims run through three vertically integrated insurer/PBM/specialty-pharmacy entities.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Pharmacy trend is concentrated, not broad, so a handful of clinical and contracting decisions control most of it: GLP-1 coverage policy, immunology biosimilar strategy (Stelara biosimilars launched in 2025), and specialty-pharmacy channel.
  - Rebate-guarantee PBM contracts hide this concentration. These figures are **net** of rebates, and growth is still dominated by a few products.
  - **Ties to unbundled positioning:** this supports a carved-out, pass-through PBM paired with targeted specialty strategies (biosimilar-first for Stelara and Humira, site-of-care and copay-assistance coordination for immunology, and explicit GLP-1 criteria). A bundled carrier/PBM can't easily do this.
  - **TX-APCD gap:** HPC can compute rebate-adjusted drug growth because Massachusetts collects rebate data alongside APCD claims. Texas collects neither self-funded claims nor PBM rebate data in a form employers can use. **Flag as a TX-APCD data gap.** The DOL PBM disclosure rule (covered Nov. 5) is the main route for a Texas self-funded employer to get comparable visibility into its own plan.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** a waterfall or stacked bar of 2020–2024 net pharmacy growth, with the top 10 drugs as segments and "everything else" as a thin sliver under 20%.
  - **LinkedIn take:** "Your pharmacy trend isn't 'drug prices.' It's about ten drugs. In Massachusetts, ten products drove 80%+ of net growth. Does your PBM contract have a strategy for each one?"

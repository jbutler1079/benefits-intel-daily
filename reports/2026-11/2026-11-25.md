### A 1% rise in health care prices cuts payroll and jobs at employers outside health care by ~0.4%. One price-raising hospital merger costs ~$32M in wages and ~203 jobs
- **Source:** Brot-Goldberg (UChicago), Cooper (Yale), Craig (UW–Madison), Klarnet (Harvard), Lurie and Miller (U.S. Treasury/IRS). *Who Pays for Rising Health Care Prices? Evidence from Hospital Mergers*, NBER Working Paper 32613, June 2024, revised Dec. 2024. [PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32613/w32613.pdf). Summary figures come from the Yale Tobin Center's key-facts sheet. [PDF](https://tobin.yale.edu/sites/default/files/publication-documents/2024-06/Hospital%20Mergers%20Key%20Facts.pdf)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **304 horizontal hospital mergers** from 2010 onward serve as price shocks.
  - Each employer's exposure is measured by how much its workers used the merging hospitals *before* the merger. That exposure is the instrument for the employer's health spending.
  - Claims: HCCI (Aetna, Humana, UnitedHealthcare), covering **28% of the ESI-covered U.S. population**.
  - Premiums: DOL Form 5500 filings, fully insured employers only.
  - Jobs and earnings: individual IRS tax records.
  - Mergers that meaningfully reduce competition raised prices about **5%** on average. Mergers that didn't reduce competition barely moved prices.
  - **Not peer-reviewed** (NBER working paper). I found no confirmation of a journal version.
- **The number:**
  - **A 1% rise in health care prices cuts payroll by 0.37% and headcount by a similar amount** at employers outside health care. Employers responded by cutting jobs, not by trimming wages.
  - **Merger price increases pass through to ESI premiums roughly dollar for dollar.** Employers did *not* respond by moving workers into high-deductible plans.
  - **Each $1 of extra hospital revenue from a price increase reduces non-health labor income by $1.33–$1.69.**
  - **County level:** each 1% price rise cuts labor income per capita by 0.27%, raises flows into unemployment by about 1%, cuts federal income tax withholding by 0.4%, and raises unemployment insurance payments by about 2.5%.
  - **One merger that raises prices 5%:** about **$32M in lost wages, 203 lost jobs, $6.8M in lost federal tax revenue, and one death** from suicide or overdose among workers outside health care.
  - **One year of mergers the FTC could have flagged but didn't challenge:** about **$400M in lost wages, 2,543 lost jobs, $85M in lost federal income tax, and 12–25 deaths**. From 2000 to 2020 there were more than 1,000 hospital mergers, and the FTC acted to block 13.
  - The job losses fall mostly on **middle-income workers**. There is roughly no effect above $100K, or below $20K, where workers rarely get ESI.
- **Why it matters:**
  - This is the clearest causal evidence that hospital prices are a cost of employment, not just a benefits-budget line. The authors call it a de facto payroll tax that hits mid-wage jobs hardest.
  - For a CFO, the case for controlling unit prices through direct contracts, reference-based pricing or steering is a case for protecting headcount, not just for trimming premiums.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** premiums absorbed merger price increases about one for one, and employers didn't change plan design in response. That passivity is what a self-funded plan with direct contracts or reference-based pricing is built to break. Pair this with the Nov. 24 report (CEA anti-steering estimate): mergers create the market power, and anti-steering clauses stop employers from routing around it.
  - **Caveat:** the premium data cover only fully insured Form 5500 filers. The paper does not estimate the effect for self-funded plans directly.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Stat card:** "One hospital merger that raises prices 5% = $32 million in lost wages and 203 lost jobs, outside the hospital."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "Every dollar a hospital gains from a price increase costs workers in other industries $1.33–$1.69 in pay. Health care prices are a jobs issue."
  - **Thread:** merger, then price +5%, then premiums (about 1:1), then payroll -0.37% per 1% price rise, then who gets hurt (middle-income workers).

### Texas health systems charge employers 2–3x what they need to break even. Hendrick, HCA and Baylor Scott & White run ~280–320% of Medicare against breakevens of ~100–120%
- **Source:** Maureen Hensley-Quinn, National Academy for State Health Policy (NASHP), *Texas House Select Committee on Health Affordability: Hospital Prices*, testimony slides, April 30, 2026. [PDF](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/handouts/C0592026043009001/acd8517c-8dcf-4b37-89ff-725a5dc8ff9f.PDF). Underlying data: NASHP Hospital Cost Tool (2024, downloaded April 2026) and RAND hospital price transparency data.
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **Commercial breakeven** is the commercial payment, as a percent of Medicare, that a hospital needs to cover its expenses after accounting for other revenue and losses. It comes from Medicare cost reports via the NASHP Hospital Cost Tool.
  - **RAND price** is the commercial (employer-plan) price as a percent of Medicare.
  - The medians are shown by Texas health system on one chart (slide 7).
  - **Only the statewide figures and the independent-hospital figures are printed as text.** I read the system-level figures off the bar chart at high resolution, so treat them as **approximate (±5 points)**. As a calibration check, my chart reading for independent hospitals matched the printed 163%/85%.
  - The slide doesn't say which RAND round it uses. The Whaley testimony at the same hearing used RAND Round 5.1 (2020–2022 claims), so RAND prices may lag the 2024 breakevens by a couple of years.
- **The number:**
  - **Texas median commercial breakeven: 110% of Medicare** (national: 112%).
  - **Texas independent hospitals:** RAND price **163%**, breakeven **85%** (printed).
  - **By system, approximate chart reads** (RAND price vs. breakeven, as % of Medicare):
    - **Hendrick Health System:** about **318% vs. 106%** (~3.0x)
    - **HCA Healthcare:** about **282% vs. 98%** (~2.9x)
    - **Baylor Scott & White:** about **296% vs. 116%** (~2.6x)
    - **Tenet Healthcare:** about **301% vs. 115%** (~2.6x)
    - **Ascension:** about **331% vs. 140%** (~2.4x)
    - **Texas Health Resources:** about **327% vs. 165%** (~2.0x)
    - **Methodist Health System (Dallas):** about **305% vs. 156%** (~2.0x)
    - **Houston Methodist:** about **303% vs. 195%** (~1.6x)
    - **Memorial Hermann:** about **248% vs. 162%** (~1.5x)
  - **Market structure (same deck):** **96% of Texas hospitals** are in highly concentrated markets (HHI > 1,800), against about 94% nationally (Yale Health Care Affordability Lab, 2025). **63 Texas hospitals became system-owned since 2000.**
- **Why it matters:**
  - Breakeven is the number to bring to a direct-contracting negotiation, because it reframes "what's a fair price?" in the system's own cost-report terms.
  - At several of the largest Texas systems, employer plans pay roughly 2.5–3x what the system needs to cover its costs. A direct contract at 150–200% of Medicare would still pay these systems well above breakeven. That answers the "you'll bankrupt the hospital" objection.
  - The spread varies a lot by system: about 1.5x at Memorial Hermann against about 3x at Hendrick and HCA. That variation tells a Texas employer where steering or a direct contract has the most room.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** breakeven plus RAND price is the standard NASHP/RAND pairing for setting reference-based price targets. A carrier-bundled plan never shows the employer either number.
  - **TX-APCD data gap, flagged prominently:** the RAND prices behind this chart come from employer-submitted and transparency data, not TX-APCD. TX-APCD excludes self-funded ERISA plans, so Texas cannot produce this price-versus-breakeven comparison for self-funded employers from state data. Texas self-funded plans pay these prices and can't see them in the state's own database. (The TX-APCD research dataset released Jan. 21, 2026, covers 2019–2024 for fully insured plans only.)
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** paired bars (breakeven vs. employer price) for 6–8 named Texas systems, captioned "What Texas hospitals need vs. what Texas employers pay." Label the values as approximate chart reads, and link the NASHP source PDF.
  - **Stat card:** "Texas independent hospitals: 85% of Medicare to break even. Employers pay 163%."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "A direct contract at 175% of Medicare isn't a lowball. For most Texas systems it's still well above what their own cost reports say they need."

### Employer-plan spending rose 18.8% from 2019–2023, and 17.7 points of it was price; utilization added just 1.8%
- **Source:** Health Care Cost Institute (HCCI), annual Health Care Cost and Utilization Report (2023 data), as covered by Managed Healthcare Executive (Oct 13, 2026).
- **Methodology/sample:** Commercial claims for about 30 million people with employer-based coverage; data end in 2023. Drug net prices are HCCI estimates using public Medicare rebate information, which the article says carries caveats (rebate amounts and drug mix differ). I read the trade-press summary, not the HCCI report itself, so figures should be checked against the report before posting.
- **The number:** Per-person annual spending rose from $5,676 to $6,743 (+18.8%): prices +17.7%, utilization +1.8%. Hospitals (inpatient + outpatient) = 46% of spending. Category growth 2019–2023: Rx +40% pre-rebate ($1,153 to $1,613), about +27% after estimated rebates and coupons ($917 to $1,162); outpatient +17%; professional +15%; inpatient +6%. Out-of-pocket rose about 10% ($837 to $923).
- **Why it matters:** Cost growth in employer plans is a price story, not a "people are using too much care" story. That favors levers aimed at price (direct contracting, reference-based pricing, unbundled TPA models with hospital-rate visibility) over utilization-only tactics like higher deductibles. Hospitals at 46% of spend is where the leverage is.
- **Social angle:** Chart: stacked bar splitting 18.8% growth into price (17.7) vs. utilization (1.8). Caption: "Your plan didn't get costlier because employees used more care. Prices did it." Add a caveat that the data end in 2023.
- **Flags:** Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning (price is the lever). TX-APCD angle: none; national data, no Texas breakout read.

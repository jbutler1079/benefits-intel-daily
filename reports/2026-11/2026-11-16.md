### In 13 states, one insurer now controls at least 80% of the fully insured large-group market, up from 6 in 2011. In 45 states, three carriers hold at least 80%
- **Source:** U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Private Health Insurance: Market Concentration in 2024 Was Generally Higher Than in 2011*, GAO-27-108927, released Oct. 16, 2026. It is the seventh report in GAO's series required by the ACA.
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **Data:** CMS Medical Loss Ratio enrollment data, 2011–2024, measured in covered life-years for the 50 states plus DC. Issuers are aggregated to their parent company.
  - **Definition of "concentrated":** three or fewer issuers hold at least 80% of a state's enrollment.
  - **Self-funded plans excluded:** GAO notes the data cover fully insured business only. Self-funded plans are left out, and they cover almost two-thirds of workers with employer coverage.
- **The number:**
  - **Large group, 2011 → 2024** (fully insured; about 39.3M enrollees, the largest of the three markets):
    - States where one issuer holds at least 80%: **6 → 13**. The count was 8 as recently as 2020.
    - States where three issuers hold at least 80%: **40 → 45**.
    - Median share of the largest issuer: **55% → 64%**. Median share of the top three: **88% → 93%**.
    - Median number of issuers per state: **12 → 8**.
  - **Small group, 2011 → 2024:**
    - States where one issuer holds at least 80%: **3 → 16**.
    - Median top-three share: **98%** in 2024.
    - Median single-issuer share: **66%** in 2024, up from 50% in 2011.
    - Median number of issuers per state: **13 → 5**.
  - **Individual market:** the trend is the opposite. Single-issuer-dominated states fell from 12 in 2019 to 1 in 2024.
- **Why it matters:** For a fully insured employer, "shopping the market" increasingly means choosing among about five carriers in small group. In 16 states, one of those carriers holds at least 80% of the market. Self-funding with an unbundled TPA, direct contracts and an independent PBM is the main way to move the purchasing decision outside a market that is consolidating this fast. The report's own footnote makes the point: self-funded plans, about two-thirds of covered workers, sit outside the market being measured.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** a two-line chart of 2011–2024 counts of states where one carrier holds at least 80%, one line for small group (3 → 16) and one for large group (6 → 13). Headline: "Fully insured 'choice' is shrinking."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "In 16 states, one insurer holds 80%+ of the small-group market. If your renewal feels non-negotiable, that's because it is."
- **Flags:**
  - **Unbundled/direct-contracting positioning: strong tie.** This is federal data showing that the fully insured alternative to self-funding is getting less competitive. It also supports level-funded and unbundled options for small employers, since small group is where single-carrier dominance grew fastest (3 → 16 states).
  - **TX-APCD: indirect.** Texas's state-level numbers are in GAO's interactive map (files.gao.gov/multimedia/gao-27-108927/interactive/) but weren't pulled today. Follow-up: get Texas's top-issuer and top-three shares for small and large group. The report's blind spot (no self-funded enrollment) mirrors the TX-APCD's: neither one covers ERISA self-funded plans.

### 91% of the growth in private premiums is explained by growth in health spending, not insurer margin. Premiums rose 78% from 2011 to 2024, and markups *fell* from 18.6% to 14.9% of premium
- **Source:** Zack Cooper (Yale, NBER) and Stuart V. Craig (UW–Madison), "Health Care Spending and Insurance Premiums Among the Privately Insured," *JAMA Health Forum* 2026;7(9):e263005, published Sept. 4, 2026.
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **Design:** a retrospective study of CCIIO Medical Loss Ratio public use files, 2011–2024. It covers large group, small group and the exchanges, aggregated to state level.
  - **Markup measure:** premium minus health spending, as a percentage. It includes both profit and administrative cost.
  - **Analysis:** a bivariate regression of state premium growth on state spending growth. The authors call the R² an accounting decomposition, not a causal estimate.
  - **Self-funded plans excluded:** self-insured employers are not in the data.
- **The number:**
  - **Growth, 2011–2024:** mean premiums per person rose **$3,143 (+78.4%)**, and health spending per covered life rose **$2,844 (+84.2%)**. At general inflation, premiums would have grown only **$1,582 (+39.5%)**.
  - **Explained share:** spending growth explains **R² = 0.91** of state premium growth. The national long-difference ratio is also 91%.
  - **Markups:** up **$299** in dollars, but **down from 18.6% to 14.9%** as a share of premium. The 2024 national mean was **$936 (12.8% of premium)**.
  - **2024 range across states:**
    - Mean premium: **$5,603 (MA) to $11,438 (AK)**.
    - Premium growth since 2011: **10.9% (MA) to 163.8% (MS)**.
- **Why it matters:**
  - It undercuts a common sales pitch. Switching carriers or squeezing admin fees addresses at most the roughly 13–15% markup slice.
  - The other roughly 85% is claims cost, mostly price. Only the purchaser can change that, through what it pays providers and drug supply chains, and that means direct contracts, reference pricing, site of care and PBM terms.
  - It fits HCCI's finding (covered 2026-10) that price drove 17.7 of 18.8 points of employer-plan spending growth.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** stacked bars of 2011 vs. 2024 premium, split into spending and markup. Headline: "Your premium didn't go up because insurers got greedier. It went up because care got pricier."
  - **Thread:** "78% premium growth. 84% spending growth. Markup share fell. Where the fix actually is →"
- **Flags:**
  - **Unbundled/direct-contracting positioning: strong tie.** It is a peer-reviewed basis for "the lever is the claims dollar, not the carrier," which is the argument for unbundling and contracting directly.
  - **TX-APCD: no direct tie.** The article doesn't break out Texas.
  - **Citation caveat:** the paper reports two markup figures (14.9% from the trend analysis, 12.8% as the 2024 national mean) without reconciling them. Cite whichever matches the framing.

### On a specialty tier, 54% of large-firm workers pay coinsurance (27% on average). Copay accumulators and maximizers absorb about $7.0B a year of manufacturer assistance, roughly one-third of it
- **Source:**
  - Bryce Platt, PharmD, "The State of Employer Pharmacy Benefits in 2026: When Plan Savings Come From Higher Patient Costs," *Drug Channels*, Nov. 10, 2026.
  - Benefit-design data come from the KFF 2026 Employer Health Benefits Survey.
  - The accumulator and maximizer dollar estimates come from Drug Channels Institute's *2026 Economic Report on U.S. Pharmacies and PBMs* (section 6.2.2).
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **KFF survey:** 1,937 private and non-federal public employers with at least 10 workers.
  - **Specialty-tier figures:** limited to workers at large firms whose plans have a dedicated specialty tier.
  - **Accumulator/maximizer dollars:** DCI's own estimates, not KFF's.
  - **Comparability:** starting in 2026, KFF classifies HRA plans by their underlying plan type, so the figures aren't directly comparable with prior years.
- **The number:**
  - **Specialty tier:** **54% of workers face coinsurance**, averaging **27%**, and 41% face a copay, averaging **$110**.
  - **HSA-qualified vs. other plans:** coinsurance applies to preferred brands for **52%** of workers in HSA-qualified plans vs. **15%** in non-HSA plans. On fourth-tier drugs it is **65% vs. 40%**.
  - **Manufacturer copay support:** accumulators absorbed about **$3.0B** and maximizers about **$4.0B**, about one-third of all manufacturer copay support.
  - **Coinsurance and rebates:** coinsurance is generally calculated on the pre-rebate price, so a better rebate can lower the plan's net cost while the patient's payment at the pharmacy stays the same.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Under a coinsurance-heavy specialty design, a self-funded plan can show lower net drug spend while members pay more for the same prescription.
  - The vendor's "savings" report won't separate the two. Platt's recommended question for the PBM: for each savings line, how much reduced the plan's liability and how much shifted cost to the patient or the manufacturer?
  - The new DOL PBM fee-disclosure rule (covered 11-09) gives sponsors the data to answer it.
- **Social angle:**
  - **LinkedIn take:** "$7 billion of drugmaker copay assistance never reaches the patient's out-of-pocket max. Ask your PBM where it went."
  - **Carousel:** "4 questions to ask about specialty savings": coinsurance basis (pre- or post-rebate), accumulator/maximizer vendor fees, how savings are split between plan and member, and audit rights.
- **Flags:**
  - **Unbundled/direct-contracting positioning: strong tie.** It shows why the PBM contract terms (point-of-sale rebates, maximizer vendor fees, pass-through) matter more than the headline net-cost number.
  - **TX-APCD: no tie.**
  - **Citation caveat:** cite the $3.0B and $4.0B to DCI's 2026 Economic Report, not to KFF. I read the Drug Channels post, not the DCI report itself.

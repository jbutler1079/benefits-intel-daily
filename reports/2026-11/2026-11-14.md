### Hospital prices rose 11.8% in two years (Oct 2024 → Oct 2026), 1.7x overall inflation (6.9%). The headline "medical care" CPI rose just 4.9%, which understates what employers pay for hospital care
- **Source:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, October 2026 release (Nov 10, 2026). Index levels were retrieved from FRED (St. Louis Fed), which republishes the BLS series: CUUR0000SEMD (Hospital and related services, Dec 2024=100), CPIMEDNS (Medical care) and CPIAUCNS (All items), all not seasonally adjusted.
- **Methodology/sample:** BLS did not publish 12-month changes for October 2026, because October 2025 data were never collected during the 2025 appropriations lapse (FRED shows "." for Oct 2025). I computed the 24-month change from published NSA index levels instead. This is my own calculation from official index values, not a BLS-published figure. The CPI measures prices across all payers, so it is a proxy for commercial hospital prices, not a direct measure of them.
- **The number:**
  - **Hospital and related services:** 99.706 → 111.493 = **+11.8% over 24 months**, about **5.7% a year**.
  - **Medical care (headline):** 567.870 → 595.467 = **+4.9%**, about 2.4% a year.
  - **All items CPI-U:** 315.664 → 337.511 = **+6.9%**, about 3.4% a year.
  - **October 2026 alone:** hospital services **+0.8%** in one month, seasonally adjusted (BLS), the largest monthly rise in the medical components.
- **Why it matters:** Hospital prices are rising at about 1.7 times general inflation. The headline medical-care CPI looks benign only because other components pull it down, such as the health-insurance index, which fell 1.8% in October. Employers whose biggest claims line is facility spend are living with the 5.7% hospital number, not the 2.4% headline. This supports putting the plan's leverage on facility pricing, through Medicare-referenced contracts, direct contracts or site-of-care steering, rather than on plan-design cost shifting.
- **Social angle:** Chart post: three bars (Hospital +11.8% / All items +6.9% / "Medical care" +4.9%) titled "The inflation number your renewal won't show you." LinkedIn take: "BLS couldn't publish a 12-month hospital inflation number this month because of last year's shutdown. So we did the two-year math: hospitals +11.8%."
- **Flags:** **Unbundled/direct-contracting positioning: moderate tie.** It is macro evidence that facility price is the cost driver to contract around. **TX-APCD: indirect.** National CPI can't show Texas hospital-specific price growth. That gap is what TX-APCD/TiC-based local analysis would fill, and it is a good setup for a "national vs. your local hospital" follow-up post. Note for citation: the hospital series is BLS "Hospital and related services" (SEMD), not the narrower "Hospital services" line.

### 41% of insulin users with employer coverage paid more than $35 out of pocket per 30-day supply in 2023, down from 58% in 2019, and state $35 caps generally don't reach self-funded plans
- **Source:** Health Care Cost Institute (HCCI), Bianca Silva Gordon, "Over 40% of Insulin Users with ESI Spend More Than $35 Out-of-pocket on 30-day Supply of Insulin," November 3, 2026.
- **Methodology/sample:** HCCI commercial claims, weighted to the national employer-sponsored insurance (ESI) population using HCCUR methodology. It covers people under 65 with at least one month of ESI enrollment in 2019–2023 who filled at least one insulin prescription (no diabetes diagnosis required). Insulin was identified using RxNorm. Out-of-pocket (OOP) cost includes deductible, coinsurance and copay, and the average OOP per 30-day supply is calculated per member per product. The total sample count isn't stated in the text, and the bracket-level percentages appear only in a figure.
- **The number:**
  - **41% of ESI insulin users** paid more than $35 OOP per 30-day supply on average in 2023, down from **more than 58% in 2019**.
  - **38% of insulin fills** were above $35 per 30-day supply in 2023, down from **52% in 2019**.
  - Medicare Part D has had a $35 cap since the IRA. More than half of states cap insulin OOP, but only for state-regulated (fully insured) commercial plans.
- **Why it matters:** State insulin caps are governed by state insurance law. ERISA preemption generally keeps them out of self-funded plans, so self-funded sponsors are the employers most likely to still have members above $35. Capping insulin cost-sharing (or adding it to a preventive-drug list) is low-cost, highly visible to members, and can improve adherence. It is also a good test of whether the PBM contract passes the net price through, because the member's coinsurance is often charged on the pre-rebate list price.
- **Social angle:** LinkedIn take: "Medicare caps insulin at $35. Over half of states do too. But 4 in 10 insulin users on employer plans still pay more, and if you're self-funded, the state cap probably doesn't cover your people. You can fix this in your plan document." Stat card: "41% → still paying >$35."
- **Flags:** **Unbundled/direct-contracting positioning: strong tie** for PBM and plan design. Self-funded sponsors control this directly, and point-of-sale rebate pass-through is a PBM contracting ask. **TX-APCD: no direct tie.** Texas's own insulin copay cap ($25/month, enacted 2021 for state-regulated plans) likewise does not reach ERISA self-funded plans. I recalled that from memory and didn't verify it today, so confirm the statute before posting.

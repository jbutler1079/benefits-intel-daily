# Daily Research — 2026-11-19

**Three findings met the bar today. None has appeared in this project's coverage before.**
1. **CVS Health / Aetna GLP-1 claims study (Nov. 15, 2026):** three years in, GLP-1 users saved about **$9,400** each in medical costs but cost **$13,300** more in net pharmacy spend, so each user cost about **$3,900 more** overall. Adherent users cost **more** on net (+$7,300), not less. CVS puts the break-even net price at about **$350/month**, below today's employer net prices.
2. **Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker inflation brief (Nov. 18, 2026):** since January 2000, hospital services prices are up **314%**. That is **more than 3x** overall inflation (100%), and physicians' services rose only 84%. From 2016 to 2026, private-insurance prices rose **34.2%**, against **23.3%** for Medicare.
3. **Milliman for PhRMA, Medicare outpatient drug spend at 340B hospitals (Oct. 12, 2026):** after controlling for patient and hospital factors, drug spending per user per month was **20–52% higher** at 340B hospitals than at non-340B hospitals, with the gap generally widening. **Industry-funded; treat accordingly.**

### GLP-1s don't pay for themselves at today's prices: three years in, medical savings of $9,400 per user are outweighed by $13,300 in net drug cost. CVS's break-even is ~$350/month
- **Source:** CVS Health, *GLP-1s: Good Drugs, High Prices* white paper and press release, Nov. 15, 2026. Year-three dollar figures are from *Insurance Business* and *Forbes* (Nov. 16–18, 2026) coverage of the release.
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **Population:** commercial, fully insured Aetna members who started GLP-1s (index years 2023–2025, up to 3 years of follow-up). Each was matched to a non-user on age, conditions, prior utilization, and prior medical and pharmacy spend.
  - **Adherent subgroup:** members whose prescriptions covered at least 80% of days.
  - **Limits:** sample size was not disclosed, and the year-three cohort is smaller than earlier ones. The analysis is not peer-reviewed. It is all indications combined (no obesity-only cut). The break-even is CVS internal modeling.
- **The number:**
  - **All initiators, year 3:** medical spend **−$9,400** per member, net pharmacy spend **+$13,300**, net total cost **+$3,900**.
  - **Adherent members:** medical **−$13,000**, pharmacy **+$20,300**, net **+$7,300**. Staying on the drug increases medical savings, but drug costs rise faster.
  - **Break-even:** about **$350/month net** over 3 years for the overall study population.
  - **Price context:** Lilly's Employer Connect lists Zepbound at a flat **$449/month** net. An EBRI estimate cited in coverage puts typical net cost at **$617–$766** per 30 days. Today's prices are roughly **1.3–2.2x** the break-even.
- **Why it matters:** This is the first large multi-year claims result from a PBM-insurer that says outright "these drugs don't pay for themselves at today's price." For a self-funded employer the question is not "cover or don't cover." The question is **what net price you are paying and who controls it**. Under a carrier/Big-3 PBM model, the employer can't see the net price, so it can't tell whether it is near $350. Pass-through PBM contracts with net-cost reporting, or direct manufacturer programs like Employer Connect, are the levers that bring the price toward break-even. The source has an interest here: CVS owns Caremark, which benefits from lower list-price narratives, and the "adherent costs more" result is the counterintuitive hook. **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning (pharmacy carve-out and pass-through PBM).**
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** stacked bar for year 3: −$9,400 medical, +$13,300 Rx, = +$3,900 net. A second bar for adherent members: +$7,300.
  - **LinkedIn take:** "CVS just told employers the price at which GLP-1s break even: $350/month. Do you know what you're paying? If your PBM won't show you the net price, that's your answer."
  - **Thread hook:** "The patients who stay on GLP-1s cost employers *more*, not less."

### Hospital prices up 314% since 2000, more than 3x overall inflation (100%). Physicians' services rose only 84%, and private-insurer prices are outrunning Medicare's (+34.2% vs +23.3% since 2016)
- **Source:** Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker, *How does medical inflation compare to inflation in the rest of the economy?*, updated Nov. 18, 2026 (data through Oct. 2026).
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - BLS CPI-U component series: medical care, hospital services, physicians' services, prescription drugs, and all items, cumulative from Jan. 2000.
  - The private-vs-Medicare comparison uses BLS producer-price data for payer-specific health-service prices, Jan. 2016 to 2026.
- **The number:**
  - **Since Jan. 2000:** hospital services **+314%**, medical care **+133%**, all items **+100%**, Rx drugs **+94%**, physicians' services **+84%**.
  - **Since Jan. 2016:** hospital services **+57%**, all items **+42%**, medical care **+31%**, physicians' services **+19%**, Rx drugs **+12%**.
  - **By payer, 2016–2026:** private-insurance prices **+34.2%**, against Medicare **+23.3%**. The gap is **~11 points** in a decade.
  - **Weight in the index:** hospital services are the largest component of the medical-care CPI, at **26%**.
- **Why it matters:** Over 26 years, hospital prices rose almost **4x** as fast as physician prices. That is the strongest long-run case that facility price, not doctor fees or utilization, is where employer dollars leak. The private-vs-Medicare split shows the gap is **widening**, so a Medicare-referenced contract (RBP or direct contract at a fixed % of Medicare) locks in a slower-growing benchmark. Carrier-negotiated rates have been compounding about 1 point a year faster. **Ties directly to unbundled/RBP/direct-contracting positioning.** This adds to (rather than repeats) the Nov. 14 BLS two-year hospital CPI item: it gives the 26-year and payer-split view.
  - **TX-APCD gap:** these are national CPI/PPI series. No public Texas dataset shows commercial vs. Medicare price growth for self-funded plans, because TX-APCD excludes ERISA plans. **Flag as a TX-APCD data gap.** TiC files are the only local substitute.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** line chart from 2000 to 2026, with hospital services (+314%) far above everything else and physicians (+84%) below overall inflation.
  - **LinkedIn take:** "Your doctor isn't the problem. Since 2000, physician prices rose 84%. Hospital prices rose 314%. Contract around the facility."
  - **Follow-up post:** "Private-insurer prices +34% vs Medicare +23% since 2016: why pegging to Medicare protects you from compounding."

### At 340B hospitals, Medicare outpatient drug spending per user is 20–52% higher than at comparable non-340B hospitals, and the gap is widening
- **Source:** Milliman (Klaisner, Nelson, Ren, LeRoy), *Medicare hospital outpatient drug spend at 340B participating hospitals*, Oct. 12, 2026. Commissioned by PhRMA. Highlighted in the Drug Channels November 2026 roundup (Nov. 17).
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - Medicare Part B fee-for-service hospital outpatient drug claims over a 6-year window ending in 2024.
  - A multivariate regression isolates the effect of 340B participation, controlling for patient traits (age, sex, dual status, disease group, hospice/institutional status) and hospital traits (teaching status, bed size, urban/rural, years in 340B).
  - Claims were repriced to ASP+6% to strip out the 340B payment-cut years. It replicates the approach of a 2015 GAO study.
- **The number:**
  - Per-user-per-month drug spending is **20–52% higher** at 340B hospitals, with the differential generally rising year over year.
  - **Drivers:** claims per user **15–32% higher** (more drugs given), and spend per claim **6–41% higher** (costlier drugs chosen).
- **Why it matters:** Even when price is held at the same Medicare rate, 340B hospitals give **more and pricier** drugs per patient. Commercial plans don't get Medicare's ASP-based pricing; they pay markups on top (RAND has found ~281% of ASP). So self-funded plans pay more per drug **and** buy more drugs at these sites. The discount the hospital receives does not reach the plan. This supports **site-of-care steering for infusions** (home infusion, independent infusion centers, white-bagging) and the push for 340B claim-level reporting. **Caveat: PhRMA-funded and opposed to 340B; this is Medicare data, not commercial.** Earlier Milliman commercial work found a similar direction. **Ties to unbundled positioning (carve-out of specialty-drug site of care).**
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** two bars, "drugs per patient +15–32%" and "cost per drug +6–41%", building to "+20–52% total."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "340B was supposed to stretch hospital resources. In Medicare data, it looks like it stretches the prescription pad. Ask where your infusions happen." Pair it with the Nov. 17 ASP−28% item for a two-post 340B series.

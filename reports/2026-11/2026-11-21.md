# Daily Research — 2026-11-21

**Two findings met the bar today. Both are primary releases from this week that this project has not covered before:**

1. **Higher commercial physician prices are tied to more medical debt in collections.** A *JAMA Network Open* study (Urban Institute, Nov. 18) covers 479 local markets. A one-standard-deviation rise in the commercial-to-Medicare ratio for professional services was associated with medical-debt prevalence about **14% higher than the 2020 average**.
2. **The AMA's 2026 insurer-competition study (Nov. 17)** finds **95% of metro commercial markets highly concentrated**, the same share as in 2014. In **47%** of metros, one insurer holds at least half the market.

A sweep of KFF, Peterson-KFF, HCCI, EBRI, RAND, GAO, FTC, Drug Channels and Texas sources (TX-APCD, Texas 2036, Baker Institute) turned up nothing else new and qualifying. Per CLAUDE.md, nothing weaker was added as padding.

### Where commercial physician prices rose relative to Medicare, more adults had medical debt in collections. A 1-SD price increase was associated with ~1.3 points more debt prevalence, ~14% of the 2020 average
- **Source:** Blavin, Braga, Pugazhendhi & Wu (Urban Institute), "Commercial Health Care Prices and Medical Debt in the US," *JAMA Network Open* 2026;9(11):e2643409, published Nov. 18, 2026.
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **Design:** a cross-sectional, ecologic first-difference regression covering **479 geozips** (areas built from 3-digit ZIP codes) between 2020 and 2023, weighted by number of consumers.
  - **Price data:** FAIR Health's National Private Insurance Claims database. Commercial prices are expressed as ratios to Medicare for the top 45 professional and outpatient CPT codes and for inpatient MS-DRGs.
  - **Debt data:** the Urban Institute Credit Panel, a 4% random sample of credit files (about 10M adults), measured Aug. 2020 and Aug. 2023.
  - **Controls:** ACS uninsurance, income and disability; Medicaid expansion; state debt-policy changes; hospital closures; and COVID and economic measures.
  - **Caveat:** this is an association at the community level. It is not causal and not linked to individual patients or benefit designs.
- **The number:**
  - **Per 1-unit rise in the professional commercial-to-Medicare ratio:** medical debt in collections over $500 rose **+4.3 percentage points** unadjusted (P<.001) and **+1.9 points** with full controls (P=.007).
  - **Per 1-SD rise (0.30 units):** about **+1.3 points**, or **~14% of the 2020 mean prevalence** of 9.0%.
  - **Debt over $1,000:** the estimates were similar, at +4.1 points unadjusted and +2.0 points adjusted.
  - **Outpatient and inpatient facility ratios:** mostly **not statistically significant**. The signal is in physician and professional prices.
  - **Context:** overall prevalence of collections over $500 fell from **9.0% to 5.0%** between 2020 and 2023, largely because of credit-bureau reporting changes. Mean price ratios were roughly flat.
- **Why it matters:**
  - This is one of the first studies to tie **negotiated commercial prices**, not just deductibles, to downstream financial harm to households.
  - Employers that send cost growth to workers through higher deductibles and coinsurance are passing those price differences straight to employees' credit files.
  - The physician-price result fits the vertical-integration story. Hospital-employed and PE-rolled-up practices raise professional rates, and the patient's cost-sharing share rises with them.
  - **Ties to direct-contracting positioning.** Contracting physician and professional services at a fixed multiple of Medicare caps the exact price lever this study associates with debt. This is a "financial wellness" argument, not just a plan-cost one.
  - **TX-APCD gap:** FAIR Health includes some self-funded claims, but TX-APCD excludes self-funded ERISA plans. A Texas employer cannot reproduce this price-to-debt link for its own population from public state data. **Flag as a TX-APCD data gap.**
- **Social angle:**
  - **LinkedIn take:** "High deductibles aren't the only thing putting your employees in collections. A new JAMA study links higher commercial *prices* to more medical debt. Your network rates show up on your employees' credit reports."
  - **Chart:** a simple two-bar visual showing that a 1-SD increase in the physician price ratio means ~14% more medical debt prevalence. Caption it clearly as an association.
  - **Thread pairing:** pair it with the Nov. 20 KFF item, where 44% of large firms responded to cost increases by raising worker premiums and 7% changed networks. The cost shift has a measurable household endpoint.

### 95% of metro commercial insurance markets are still "highly concentrated," exactly the share in 2014. In 47% of metros one insurer holds at least half the market, and Blues plans lead in 84%
- **Source:** American Medical Association, *Competition in Health Insurance: A Comprehensive Study of U.S. Markets*, 2026 update (2025 data), released Nov. 17, 2026.
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **Coverage:** **385 metropolitan areas**, all 50 states and DC, using 2025 enrollment.
  - **Concentration measure:** HHI under the Dec. 2023 DOJ/FTC merger guidelines, where an HHI above 1,800 counts as "highly concentrated."
  - **Data:** the release does not name the enrollment vendor or say whether ASO/self-insured lives are included. Prior AMA editions counted commercial enrollment including self-insured; verify against the full report before citing that detail.
  - **Source caveat:** the AMA represents physicians and is an interested party in insurer-provider negotiations. The underlying market-share data are third-party enrollment counts.
- **The number:**
  - **Commercial markets:**
    - **95% (372 of 385)** of metro markets are highly concentrated, unchanged from 2014.
    - At least one insurer holds 30% or more in **90%** of metros, and 50% or more in **47% (180)**.
    - **BCBS plans** have the largest share in **84% (323)** of metros and hold **42%** of the national commercial market combined.
  - **Largest national commercial shares:** UnitedHealth Group 15%, Aetna/CVS 12%, Elevance 12%, Cigna 8%, and HCSC (the parent of BCBS Texas) 8%.
  - **Least competitive states:** Alabama, Kentucky, Hawaii, Louisiana and Michigan lead the list. Six states hit 10-year concentration peaks: CT, IL, IN, MT, SD and WV.
- **Why it matters:**
  - For a self-funded employer, carrier concentration shows up mainly in the **ASO network and the TPA market**. When one carrier's network is the only one with deep discounts in a metro, the employer has little leverage on admin fees, data access or steering.
  - This is the structural reason unbundling exists: separating claims administration from network rental and contracting directly removes dependence on the dominant carrier's network.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** use it alongside the GAO state-level report covered Nov. 16, where one carrier holds 80%+ of the fully insured large-group market in 13 states. Those are two independent datasets with the same conclusion.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Map:** shade metros by whether one insurer holds 50%+ share, and overlay "Blues #1 in 84% of metros."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "In 2014, 95% of metro insurance markets were highly concentrated. In 2025: still 95%. A decade of 'more choice' produced none. Self-funding plus unbundled administration is how employers get choice the market won't give them."
  - **Pairing:** run it after the Nov. 16 GAO item in a two-part "who you're really negotiating with" series.

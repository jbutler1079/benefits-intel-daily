### Houston ER doctors' arbitration awards: median $1,484 for a mid-level ER visit, about 21x Medicare and 6x the highest in-network rate any of four big carriers pays. For the top two ER visit levels, more than half of awards beat the highest contracted fee
- **Source:** Scott Phillips and Adam Singleton, "No Surprises Act: Independent dispute resolution awards and healthcare transparency data," Milliman brief, **Nov. 18, 2026**. [Brief](https://www.milliman.com/en/insight/no-surprises-act-dispute-resolution-awards-transparency-data)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **IDR data:** CMS's federal IDR public-use file for **2025 Houston disputes** on ER physician E/M codes 99283, 99284 and 99285. That is **35,000+ disputes**: 4,920 for 99283, ~24,300 for 99284 and 6,454 for 99285. The authors removed clear errors but did not fully audit the file.
  - **Comparison rates:** Transparency in Coverage (TiC) in-network fee schedules for four carriers' group products, via Milliman Transparent and Turquoise Health data: Aetna Choice POS II, BCBSTX Blue Choice PPO, Cigna OAP and UHC Choice Plus. Rates were also compared with 2025 Houston Medicare allowed fees ($71, $122 and $176 for the three codes).
  - **Scope:** the physician professional fee only, with no facility fee. The brief doesn't report QPAs.
- **The number:**
  - **99283 (moderate ER visit):** the median prevailing IDR offer was **$1,484 (2,086% of Medicare)**. The 75th percentile was **$3,167 (4,453%)**, the 90th **$5,947 (8,362%)**, and the maximum **$8,625**.
  - **In-network comparison for 99283:** the highest in-network fee posted by any of the four carriers was **$241 (Cigna, ~339% of Medicare)**. The median across all the carriers' posted fees was **~$126 (~177% of Medicare)**. Only the **25th-percentile** award ($255) is in the same range as contracted rates. The median award is about **6x** the highest in-network rate, and the 75th percentile about **13x**.
  - **99284 and 99285:** median awards were **$658 (541% of Medicare)** and **$956 (542%)**, with maximums of **$24,180** and **$15,947**. Per the authors, **more than half** of awards on both codes were above the highest contracted commercial fee.
  - **Authors' conclusion:** IDR outcomes "appear disconnected from those market forces." They suggest arbitrators (IDREs) consider the full range of TiC rates when choosing between the two offers.
- **Why it matters:**
  - The No Surprises Act applies to self-funded ERISA plans, so these awards are paid out of the employer's claims account. ER physicians are the specialty members can't choose and plans can't steer.
  - At these award levels, an out-of-network ER group in Houston has an incentive to stay out of network and arbitrate rather than sign a contract.
  - This is local and code-level. It complements the national total in EBRI Issue Brief 669 ($8.39B in 2025), which we covered Nov. 23.
  - **TX-APCD data gap:** this is Texas-specific. TX-APCD doesn't capture what self-funded plans end up paying after IDR, so this kind of exposure doesn't show up in state cost data. Flag prominently.
  - **Ties to unbundled positioning:** an unbundled TPA or a plan advocate can track which ER groups bill out-of-network and contest their IDR cases with TiC rate evidence (the approach Milliman recommends). A carrier ASO usually handles IDR in bulk, and the employer may never see it.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Stat card:** "Houston ER visit (99283). Medicare: $71. Highest in-network rate: $241. Median arbitration award: $1,484."
  - **Chart:** one bar each for Medicare, median in-network, highest in-network, and IDR award at the 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentiles. The jump after the 25th percentile is the story.
  - **LinkedIn take:** "The No Surprises Act protected patients. In Houston, the plan pays 6x the best contracted rate instead. If you're self-funded, ask your TPA how many IDR cases you lost last year, and at what multiple of in-network."

### For the same Chicago heart failure admission, UnitedHealthcare's negotiated rates now range 3.3x ($11,204–$36,950), up from 2.5x in 2024. The highest-priced hospital has exactly median mortality, and 47 of 49 hospitals raised rates
- **Source:** Katie Patton, Jim Browne, Maura Eshleman and Allison Oakes, "Price Transparency Enables but Does Not Ensure Value for Money in Hospital Care," Trilliant Health, **Oct. 22, 2026**. [Study](https://www.trillianthealth.com/market-research/studies/price-transparency-enables-but-does-not-ensure-value-for-money-in-hospital-care)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **Hospitals:** **49 short-term acute care hospitals** in the Chicago-Naperville-Elgin CBSA, comparing 2024 with 2026.
  - **Price:** UnitedHealthcare commercial negotiated rates for **MS-DRG 291** (heart failure and shock with MCC), from Trilliant's health plan price transparency dataset.
  - **Quality:** CMS risk-standardized **30-day heart failure mortality**, which is mostly based on Medicare FFS patients.
  - **Limits:** this is one payer and one condition in one market. Trilliant is a commercial data vendor, though the underlying data are public TiC files and CMS measures.
- **The number:**
  - **Price range:** **$11,204 to $36,950** in 2026 (**3.3x**), up from $10,202–$25,678 (**2.5x**) in 2024.
  - **Median rate:** **$16,689 → $19,105** (+14.5%). The median hospital-level increase was 6.9%.
  - **Hospitals raising rates:** **47 of 49**. Eleven raised rates by more than 20%, seven by more than 30%, and one by **101.8%**.
  - **Mortality:** ranged **5.7%–13.0%** (2.3x). The market median improved from 10.3% to 8.9%.
  - **Highest price, median quality:** the **$36,950** hospital had **8.9%** mortality, exactly the market median.
  - **Price barely predicts quality:** correlation between rate and mortality was only **r = −0.26 (2024) and −0.32 (2026)**.
  - **Paying more for lower value:** six hospitals improved mortality but raised rates **9.7%–56.6%**, enough that their overall value score fell.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Heart failure admissions are unplanned: patients go where the ambulance takes them. That makes network selection the decision that matters, rather than steering patients at the point of care.
  - When price explains only about a tenth of the variation in mortality (r² ≈ 0.10), "you get what you pay for" isn't a defensible reason to pay the top rate.
  - **Ties to direct-contracting positioning:** this is a ready-made case for building a network on price *and* outcomes. Use TiC rates plus CMS Care Compare mortality to exclude or tier the high-price, average-quality hospitals.
  - **Texas application:** the same method could be run on Texas hospitals with public TiC files and Care Compare. We have no Texas version yet, and TX-APCD doesn't link price to outcomes.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Scatter plot:** negotiated rate (x) vs. 30-day mortality (y) for the 49 hospitals, with a near-flat trend line. (Recreate from the study's figure; Trilliant doesn't name the hospitals.)
  - **Stat card:** "Same heart failure admission, same insurer, same city: $11,204 or $36,950. The $36,950 hospital's death rate: exactly average."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "Price transparency data shows the price spread getting wider, not narrower. It's not working as a market on its own. Someone has to use it, and for a self-funded plan, that's the network decision."

### Commercial plans pay hospital-based psychiatric care only 1.37x Medicare, against ~2.5x for hospital care overall. Behavioral health is paid 16%–59% less than physical health in every state, and the same therapy hour pays $339 at Cigna vs. $174 at UHC
- **Source:**
  - **Primary:** Kyle King, Joshua Skydel, Joseph Ross and Joshua Wallach, "Commercial Price Variation for 11 Outpatient-Based Psychiatric Services," *JAMA Network Open* 2026;9(1):e2552939, **Jan. 20, 2026**. [Article (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12820733/)
  - **Corroborating:** The Kennedy Forum and Third Horizon, **Mental Health Parity Index**, released **Apr. 2026** (Kennedy Forum release dated Apr. 14; Third Horizon gives Apr. 20). [Third Horizon summary](https://www.thirdhorizon.com/insights/parity-has-been-the-law-since-2008-the-data-to-measure-it-is-finally-here)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - **King et al.:**
    - **Data and sample:** Turquoise Health hospital price transparency data as of June 2025, totaling **94,228 commercial rates** from **1,398 hospitals** in 49 states and DC.
    - **Services:** 11 psychiatric CPT codes: psychotherapy, family and group therapy, diagnostic evaluation, ECT and TMS.
    - **Payers:** Aetna, BCBS, Cigna and UHC.
    - **Method:** rates are geographically adjusted, trimmed at the 1st and 99th percentiles, and compared with Medicare, cash and list prices.
    - **Limit:** hospital settings only, which excludes independent therapists.
  - **Parity Index:**
    - **Data:** TiC in-network files from Aetna, BCBS, Cigna and UHC.
    - **Method:** compares in-network access and reimbursement for behavioral health against physical health within the same networks. It was piloted in Illinois.
    - **Limit:** I couldn't read the full methodology, including the data period and the exact codes compared.
- **The number:**
  - **King et al. (price levels):**
    - **Ratio to Medicare:** a median **1.37x Medicare** (IQR 1.02–1.47). It ranges from **1.08x** for 30-minute psychotherapy to 1.67x for initial TMS.
    - **Cash and list:** commercial rates are 1.12x the hospital's cash price and 0.59x list price.
  - **King et al. (variation):**
    - **Between hospitals:** the 90th-to-10th percentile ratio is **4.16x** overall and **11.98x** for ECT.
    - **Within a hospital:** it's only 1.15x. Price depends far more on which hospital than on which insurer.
    - **Insurers:** Cigna had the highest median rate for 8 of 11 services. A 60-minute psychotherapy session pays **$339 at Cigna vs. $174 at UHC**.
  - **Parity Index:**
    - **Payment gap:** in **all 50 states**, outpatient behavioral health pays less than outpatient physical health in the same plans, by **16%–59%** depending on the network.
    - **Access gap:** **43 states** and **7 in 10 counties** show potential gaps in in-network behavioral health access.
    - **Benchmarks:** none of the four insurers meets parity benchmarks nationally.
  - **For contrast:** RAND Round 5.1 put commercial hospital prices overall at ~254% of Medicare, and Texas at 259% (covered earlier).
- **Why it matters:**
  - Hospital prices are high almost everywhere else, but psychiatric care is paid close to Medicare. This is the one service line where the problem is underpayment.
  - Low in-network rates likely help explain why behavioral health patients go out of network about 3x as often (RTI, 2024). That out-of-network use costs the plan and the member more.
  - It's also a compliance issue. Under MHPAEA, plan sponsors are responsible for comparative NQTL analyses, and reimbursement rates and network adequacy are the areas DOL is pressing on (FAB 2026-03, covered earlier).
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** a self-funded plan can set its own behavioral health rates (e.g., a percent of Medicare above the carrier's) and contract directly with therapy groups. Paying a little more in-network can cost less than members going out of network, and it reduces parity risk. A carrier network pays one rate schedule for everyone.
  - **TX angle:** neither source breaks out Texas in what I could read. The Parity Index has state and county views worth pulling for Texas metros.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** commercial-to-Medicare ratios side by side: inpatient/outpatient hospital overall (~2.5x) vs. psychiatric services (1.37x) vs. 30-min psychotherapy (1.08x).
  - **Stat card:** "Same hour of therapy: $339 at one insurer, $174 at another."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "Employers are told mental health is a priority, but carrier networks pay therapists barely above Medicare while paying hospitals 2.5x. That's why your employees can't find an in-network therapist, and why it's a parity compliance issue for the plan sponsor."

# Daily Research — 2026-11-15

**Two findings met the bar today. Neither was published this week, but neither has appeared in this project's coverage before. First, a JAMA Network Open study built on Transparency in Coverage files: for the same cardiac device implant, insurers pay facilities very different amounts, with UnitedHealthcare's median facility price for an ICD insertion 5.4x Aetna's. Second, NYC's 2026 Healthcare Accountability Report, written by a public purchaser about its own self-insured plan, finds that price, not utilization, drives cost growth: about 70% of hospital spend goes to five academic systems, and outpatient spend grew roughly 3x as fast as inpatient. This Sunday's sweep found no new RAND, NASHP, KFF, HCCI, EBRI, GAO, FTC, BLS or Texas release in the past 7 days.**

### Same implant, different carrier: UnitedHealthcare's median facility price for an ICD insertion was $36,269, against $6,674 at Aetna (5.4x). Facility fees varied more than physician fees for 25 of 26 cardiology services
- **Source:** Philips, Dhruva & Whaley, "Commercial Price Variation for Common Cardiovascular Services Across 4 Major US Insurers," *JAMA Network Open* 2026;9(7):e2623326, published July 16, 2026.
- **Methodology/sample:** A cross-sectional study of April 2025 Transparency in Coverage (TiC) negotiated-rate files, compiled by Clarify Health, reflecting 2023 contract-year rates. The four insurers in the study (BCBS, UnitedHealthcare, Cigna and Aetna) cover about 78% of the commercial market. It covers 32 common cardiology CPT codes, with about 6.7M professional price points from 51,568 physicians and 104,563 facility price points from 4,128 facilities. Prices are weighted by provider claim volume. Medicare Advantage was excluded, and the authors could not link prices to quality.
- **The number:**
  - **ICD insertion, facility median:** **$36,269 at UnitedHealthcare vs. $6,674 at Aetna**, 5.4x.
  - **Pacemaker insertion, facility median:** $17,678 at UnitedHealthcare vs. $6,366 at Aetna, 2.8x.
  - **Facility prices vary more than physician prices:** the median interquartile ratio (75th ÷ 25th percentile) was **2.56 for facility fees vs. 1.78 for professional fees**. For an echocardiogram, the facility fee's coefficient of variation was **3.87, against 0.61** for the physician fee.
  - **Price index by insurer:** ranged from 0.39 (Aetna electrophysiology, 61% below the four-insurer average) to 1.46 (UnitedHealthcare facility stress testing). BCBS's electrophysiology facility prices were more than **3x** Aetna's.
- **Why it matters:** For an employer, choosing a carrier or ASO network is a price decision for each service, not just an admin-fee decision. The spread is in the facility fee, not the physician fee, so the leverage is in facility contracting, site of care and Medicare-referenced or direct facility rates. The network-discount percentage a carrier advertises shows none of this.
- **Social angle:** Chart post: paired bars for ICD and pacemaker facility prices by insurer, titled "Same device. Same procedure. Up to 5x the price, depending on whose card you hold." LinkedIn take: "The physician isn't the price problem. The facility fee is: 2.56x spread vs. 1.78x."
- **Flags:** **Unbundled/direct-contracting positioning: strong tie.** This is peer-reviewed evidence that a bundled carrier network does not deliver a consistent price, and that the variation sits in facility fees, which direct or referenced contracts target. **TX-APCD: indirect.** The study's state cut (CPT 93458) doesn't single out Texas. Pulling the same codes from Texas TiC files is a ready follow-up, and it shows what TiC data can do while the TX-APCD's commercial price data is limited. Caveats for citation: the rates are 2023 contract-year, and TiC "price points" are negotiated rates, not paid claims.

### NYC's own self-insured plan: price, not use, drives cost growth. About 70% ($3B) of 2025 hospital spend went to five academic systems, and outpatient spend rose 13–17% in a year against 4–5% for inpatient
- **Source:** NYC Department of Health and Mental Hygiene, Office of Healthcare Accountability (created by Local Law 78 of 2023), *2026 Healthcare Accountability Report*, released September 2026. Findings are as summarized in the NYC Council press release of Sept. 28, 2026. The figures of 336% and 76% of Medicare come from secondary coverage (Employer Coverage Substack, Oct. 19, 2026), and I could not verify them against the PDF.
- **Methodology/sample:** The report covers City health plan spending for employees, retirees and dependents (the City plan covers about 900k lives through Anthem/GHI). It combines hospital and payer TiC price-transparency data with public hospital and insurer financials. Prices are actual allowed amounts, expressed as a percent of Medicare allowed. The report PDF couldn't be retrieved in full this run (it exceeds the fetch-tool size limit), so per-hospital tables weren't checked.
- **The number:**
  - **Concentration:** about **70% ($3B)** of the City plans' 2025 hospital spend went to five academic systems: Montefiore, Mount Sinai, NewYork-Presbyterian, Northwell and NYU Langone.
  - **Site-of-care shift:** City plan **outpatient spend rose 13–17% in one year**, concentrated at two systems, vs. **4–5% for inpatient**.
  - **Price spread (secondary):** NewYork-Presbyterian averaged **336% of Medicare**, vs. **76%** at NYC Health + Hospitals.
  - **Same-service spread:** C-section **$7,000–$64,000**; ER visit **$210–$6,000**.
  - **City premium spend:** **$10.03B** in FY2025, up 15% over two years.
- **Why it matters:** This is one of the largest public self-insured purchasers publishing its own claims evidence for the case private employers make: cost growth is a price problem and a hospital-concentration problem, and the fastest growth is in outpatient hospital spend, where site-neutral and facility-fee strategies apply. A 4.4x spread (336% vs. 76% of Medicare) inside one city shows the size of the steerage opportunity.
- **Social angle:** LinkedIn take: "New York City audited its own health plan. 70% of hospital dollars went to 5 systems, and outpatient spend grew 3x faster than inpatient." Thread: walk through the five numbers above, ending with "What would your claims data show?"
- **Flags:** **Unbundled/direct-contracting positioning: strong tie** (price vs. utilization, concentration, outpatient facility fees). **TX-APCD: direct tie.** NYC built an office (budget about $2M, 15 staff per 2025 coverage) to do the purchaser-side price analysis that Texas public purchasers (ERS, TRS) could do with TX-APCD/TiC data but don't publish. It is a strong "why doesn't Texas do this?" angle.

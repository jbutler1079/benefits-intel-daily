1. **Same infused drug, 102% more at the hospital.** Hospital outpatient departments (HOPDs) are paid on average 102% more per unit than physician offices for the same physician-administered drug. Paying office rates would save employer plans **$12.7B a year (~$101 per member)**, and almost all of that saving goes to the plan, not the patient (EBRI Issue Brief 666, Sept. 24, 2026).
2. **Vermont's own self-insured public plans paid 289% of Medicare for hospital care.** Capping at 200% would have saved **~$400M over 5.75 years** (2018–Q3 2023). Vermont's legislature then passed a 250%-of-Medicare cap for fully insured plans in 2026 (Green Mountain Care Board / Health Management Associates, Dec. 2024; S.190, 2026).
3. **PBM-owned pharmacies aren't always the expensive option.** In Medicare Part D, insurer- and PBM-owned pharmacies were cheaper for at least 94% of the top 100 generics. But for about half of the 20 highest-cost brand drugs, cost sharing was **up to ~$340 higher** at owned pharmacies (GAO-26-107393, released Oct. 5, 2026).

### Same drug, different building: HOPDs are paid 102% more per unit than physician offices for physician-administered drugs. Paying office rates would save employer plans $12.7B a year (~$101 per member), almost none of it at the patient's point of service
- **Source:** Paul Fronstin (EBRI) and M. Christopher Roebuck (RxEconomics), *Location, Location, Location: Spending Differences for Physician-Administered Outpatient Medications by Site-of-Treatment*, EBRI Issue Brief No. 666, Sept. 24, 2026. [PDF](https://www.ebri.org/docs/default-source/pbriefs/ebri_ib_666_siteneutrtreatment-24sept26.pdf?sfvrsn=a14f0a2f_1)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - Claims come from the Merative MarketScan Commercial Database.
  - The sample is adults aged 18–64 continuously enrolled in employer plans: **10.9M people in 2024** and 9.6M in 2023. Capitated plans are excluded.
  - It covers the **106 highest-spending physician-administered outpatient drugs** (HCPCS J, Q and S codes). These drugs make up 51% of such claims and **77% of spending** on drugs billed under the medical benefit.
  - Prices are compared using the per-unit allowed amount (plan plus patient payment), HOPD versus physician office (PO).
  - The trend analysis re-runs EBRI's 2021 set of 70 drugs for each year from 2019 to 2024.
  - **Funding disclosure:** funders include PhRMA, Johnson & Johnson, Merck, BCBSA and MedImpact.
- **The number:**
  - **59% of administrations happen in HOPDs**, against 31% in physician offices and 9% in other settings, mostly the patient's home.
  - **HOPDs were paid more for 93 of 106 drugs.** The HOPD per-unit premium averages **102%**, with a median of **64%**.
  - The **median per-patient annual difference is $5,531**. For one oncology drug it reaches **$135,306**.
  - **Savings at office rates:** **$9.8B a year** for the 106 drugs ($77.40 per member per year). That rises to **$12.7B a year** extrapolated to all such drugs ($101.05 per member per year), about **1% of total employer-plan health spending**.
  - **Who keeps the savings:** among these claims, **90% had no deductible payment, 80% had no coinsurance and 97% had no copay**. Deductibles were 1.1% of spending and coinsurance 1.7%.
  - **Trend:** the median HOPD markup fell from **98% (2019) to 70% (2024)**. That was mostly because **physician-office prices rose ~17%**, not because hospital prices fell.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Patients on these drugs have usually already hit their deductible and out-of-pocket maximum. As a result, benefit design and cost sharing can't steer them, and the plan bears almost all of the HOPD markup.
  - Only contracting can capture these savings: site-of-care programs, site-neutral rates, or direct deals with infusion centers and physician offices. Plan design can't.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** this is a ready-made argument for carving specialty infusion out to a contracted lower-cost site. A carrier that owns or partners with hospital systems has little incentive to do that.
  - **Caveat:** the narrowing gap reflects physician-office prices rising, and the authors note that hospital purchases of physician practices reduce purchasers' leverage.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Stat card:** "Same drug. Same dose. 102% more if it's infused in a hospital outpatient department."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "90% of these claims had no deductible payment. Your plan, not your employee, pays the hospital markup on infusions, so your plan is the one that has to fix it."
  - **Chart:** EBRI's markup trend line (98%, then 84%, 77%, 69%, 70%), captioned "The gap is closing because doctors' offices got more expensive."

### Vermont's teacher and state-employee plans paid hospitals 289% of Medicare. A 200% cap would have saved ~$400M over 2018–2023, and Vermont then passed a 250% cap for fully insured plans
- **Source:**
  - Green Mountain Care Board (GMCB), *2024 Reference-Based Pricing and Data Analysis Report*, submitted Dec. 16, 2024, under Act 113 of 2024. The analysis was done by Health Management Associates (HMA). [PDF](https://gmcboard.vermont.gov/sites/gmcb/files/documents/GMCB_RBP_Report_Draft_12_16_24_PDFA.pdf)
  - Follow-on legislation: Act 68 of 2025 and S.190 (2026), "as passed by both House and Senate." [PDF](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/BILLS/S-0190/S-0190%20As%20Passed%20by%20Both%20House%20and%20Senate%20Official.pdf)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - The data are inpatient and outpatient hospital claims from Vermont's all-payer claims database (VHCURES), **2018 through Q3 2023**.
  - They cover two self-insured public plans: the State Employees' plan (VSEA) and the school employees' plan (VEHI), about **59,000 members** in all. They are the largest self-insured group in Vermont.
  - **Price measure:** the allowed amount, compared with the national Medicare base price.
  - **Scope:** Vermont hospitals plus Dartmouth-Hitchcock (NH); other out-of-state facilities are excluded.
  - **Caveat:** the savings are static repricing at 100% compliance, with no volume response modeled.
- **The number:**
  - **Overall: 289% of Medicare** (inpatient 278%, outpatient 293%).
  - **Prices rose every year**, from 281% in 2018 to 297% in 2022 and **306% in Q1–Q3 2023**. The school plan (VEHI) reached **326%** in 2023.
  - **Savings over the study period by cap:**
    - at **150%** of Medicare: **$565M**
    - at **180%**: **$466M**
    - at **200%**: **$400M** ($79M in 2022)
    - at **250%**: **$234M**
  - The total allowed amount was $1.117B, so **a 200% cap equals about 36% of hospital spend**.
  - **Outpatient was $321M of the $400M**, and inpatient $78M.
  - **Critical access hospitals averaged 291% of Medicare, slightly above the 289% for larger PPS hospitals.** Most of the dollar savings ($287M) still came from the PPS hospitals.
  - **What came next:**
    - **Act 68 (2025)** requires the GMCB to set hospital reference-based prices by hospital fiscal year 2027.
    - **S.190 (2026)** bars insurers from paying hospitals **more than 250% of the Medicare adjusted base rate** for fully insured qualified health plans. It also targets commercial rates above **500%** of Medicare for the first budget-ordered cuts, and bars balance billing.
    - **S.190 Sec. 4** directs a new GMCB analysis of reference-based pricing for VSEA/VEHI, **due Jan. 15, 2027**.
- **Why it matters:**
  - This is a self-insured public employer pricing its own claims against Medicare. That is exactly the exercise a private self-funded employer can do with its own claims, and the cap-by-cap table is the template.
  - A 200% cap, still well above Medicare, would have cut more than a third of hospital spend.
  - In Vermont, rural critical access hospitals weren't cheaper than large hospitals relative to Medicare, which undercuts the "rural hospitals need the margin" objection. Pair this with the access protections Oregon and Montana used.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** S.190's 250% cap applies to fully insured plans. Self-funded ERISA plans are outside a state rate cap and have to get there through their own contracts or reference-based pricing. A carrier-bundled ASO plan gets no automatic benefit.
  - **TX-APCD data gap, flagged prominently:** Vermont could run this analysis because its APCD (VHCURES) captures the public employers' self-insured claims. TX-APCD excludes self-funded ERISA plans, so Texas cannot produce the same table for its self-funded employers from state data. ERS and TRS are Texas's own large self-funded public plans; whether their claims could support a similar Texas study is worth asking.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** the savings ladder by cap (150%, 180%, 200%, 250% of Medicare) against the $1.117B actually paid.
  - **Thread:** a state priced its own teachers' hospital claims against Medicare, found 289% and rising, and then legislated a cap.
  - **LinkedIn take:** "Vermont's school plan hit 326% of Medicare in 2023. If a state can run this analysis on its own claims, so can your self-funded plan."

### PBM-owned pharmacies were cheaper for 94%+ of common generics in Part D, but for about half of the 20 costliest brand drugs, cost sharing ran up to ~$340 higher than at independent pharmacies
- **Source:** U.S. Government Accountability Office, *Medicare Part D: Payments and Contracts Between Pharmacies and Plan Sponsors That Are Vertically Integrated*, GAO-26-107393. Published Sept. 3, 2026, released Oct. 5, 2026, and reissued Oct. 6, 2026 with corrected percentages. [Report page](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107393)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - The data are **2023** CMS Part D payment and utilization data for **four large vertically integrated plan sponsors**. Together they hold more than 40% of Part D enrollment.
  - GAO compared 30-day-supply payments and cost sharing at owned pharmacies (mostly mail-order) with non-owned pharmacies (mostly retail).
  - It looked at three drug sets: the top 100 drugs by utilization (mostly generic), the top 100 by payments (mostly brand), and the 20 highest-paid drugs per 30-day supply.
  - **The sample covers Medicare only.** GAO says the results don't generalize to other sponsors or years.
- **The number:**
  - Owned pharmacies handled **~28% of Part D drug use** and **~24% of pharmacy payments** for these sponsors. (The Fast Facts section still says ~25%.)
  - **Generics:** owned pharmacies were cheaper on payment and cost sharing for **at least 94% of the top 100 drugs**. Most cost $10 or less a month.
  - **High-cost brands:** for **about half of the 20 highest-paid drugs**, cost sharing at owned pharmacies was **up to ~$340 higher**.
- **Why it matters:**
  - This is a nuanced, nonpartisan data point for employer PBM reviews. On cheap generics, the integrated PBM's own pharmacy can look like the low-cost option.
  - The exposure sits in a small number of very expensive brand drugs, which is exactly where employer spend concentrates.
  - Employers shouldn't judge a vertically integrated PBM by its generic pricing; they should audit owned-pharmacy pricing drug by drug for high-cost brands and specialty drugs.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** this supports pass-through contracts with drug-level reporting of affiliated-pharmacy pricing. The DOL PBM fee-disclosure rule (covered Nov. 9) requires disclosure of compensation to affiliates.
  - **Caveat:** this is Medicare Part D, not commercial. Commercial pricing could differ.
- **Social angle:**
  - **LinkedIn take:** "GAO: the PBM's own pharmacy was cheaper on 94% of common generics. On half of the 20 most expensive brand drugs, patients paid up to $340 more there. Audit the expensive drugs, not the cheap ones."
  - **Stat card:** "$10 generics, cheaper. $10,000 brands, not always."

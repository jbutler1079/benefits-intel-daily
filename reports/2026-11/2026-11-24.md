### Banning anti-steering, anti-tiering and all-or-nothing hospital contracts would cut hospital prices ~18% and employer premiums ~6.5% where those clauses bind: about $1,755 per family a year, or ~$45B nationally
- **Source:** White House Council of Economic Advisers (CEA), *Effects of Banning Hospitals' Anti-Steering, Anti-Tiering, and All-or-Nothing Contracts*, memo, June 2026. [PDF](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Effects-of-Banning-Hospitals-Anti-Steering-Anti-Tiering-and-All-or-Nothing-Contracts.pdf)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - A simulation built from published estimates, not new claims data. It adds up three channels: insurer bargaining leverage (6–10% lower prices), patient sorting to cheaper hospitals (8%, from Massachusetts tiered-network research), and rival hospitals growing over time (3–7%). It subtracts about 3 points for overlap and adds a 10% cut to affiliated-physician prices.
  - Converts price cuts to premiums using the hospital share of employer (ESI) spending (45% facility + 11.6% affiliated physicians = 56.6%) and an assumed **70% pass-through** from insurers to premiums.
  - **Assumes** the clauses bind for **24% of ESI lives** (40% of markets with viable alternatives × 60% clause prevalence). Hospital–insurer contracts are confidential, so the CEA says the 60% figure "is unobservable" and is a working assumption, based on litigation in Charlotte (Atrium), Northern California (Sutter), western NC (HCA/Mission), Columbus (OhioHealth) and New York (NewYork-Presbyterian).
  - This is a political-branch estimate supporting DOJ's pending cases. Treat it as a policy estimate, and cite the ranges.
- **The number:**
  - **Hospital and affiliated-physician prices fall 18%** in directly affected markets (range **11–26%**), about **$4,100 per inpatient admission** (on an average system-hospital commercial stay of ~$23,000).
  - **ESI premiums fall 6.5%** in those markets (range 4.1–9.2%): about **$1,755 per family** and **$606 per individual** per year (2025 dollars).
  - **National:** about **1.6%** of ESI premiums, **~$45B a year** (range $29–63B). A more conservative near-term case gives about $25B ($20–35B).
  - **By market type:** 4–7% premium reduction where a large multi-market system can't be excluded from networks; 1–2% in competitive markets. No effect at true sole-community hospitals.
  - Supporting figures it cites: **55.1%** of physicians were employed by hospitals or systems (Jan. 2024). Hospital-affiliated physicians' commercial allowed amounts run **11–26%** above independent peers.
  - Caution: page 4 of the PDF says "~$5 billion" in one sentence where the summary and range say ~$45B. It looks like a typo. Quote $45B, citing the BLUF and executive summary.
- **Why it matters:**
  - The model's biggest single lever is *patient sorting*: steering members to lower-priced hospitals through benefit design. Anti-steering and anti-tiering clauses exist to block exactly that. An employer can't design a tiered network or a direct contract around a system that its carrier has contractually promised not to steer away from.
  - The CEA's own pass-through assumption (70%) means it expects carriers to keep about 30% of hospital savings in concentrated insurer markets. A self-funded employer that pays claims directly keeps 100% of any price cut.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** a self-funded plan using a carrier's leased network inherits that carrier's contract clauses. An unbundled TPA with a direct contract or reference-based price is not bound by them. The question for employers: "Does my network contract let my plan steer at all?" Ask your TPA or carrier whether the systems in your area have anti-tiering or all-or-nothing terms.
  - **TX-APCD gap:** the CEA had to *assume* clause prevalence because contracts are confidential. Testing the effect in Texas would mean comparing commercial prices at systems with and without these terms. TX-APCD excludes self-funded ERISA plans, so Texas employers can't run that comparison on plans like their own. **Flag as a TX-APCD data gap.** (Not checked: whether Texas law already restricts any of these clauses.)
- **Social angle:**
  - **Stat card:** "18% lower hospital prices. $1,755 per family per year. That's the White House's estimate of what hospital anti-steering clauses cost employers." Show the range (11–26%) in the caption.
  - **LinkedIn take:** "Your carrier may have signed a contract that says it won't steer your employees away from the most expensive hospital system in town. Self-funded plans with their own contracts don't have to sign that."
  - **Explainer carousel:** one slide per clause (anti-steering, anti-tiering, all-or-nothing), each with a plain-English definition.

### Stop-loss carriers paid out 91.3% of premium in 2025, a six-year high. Claims over $5 million became 267% more frequent from 2020 to 2024, and claims grew faster than premiums every year on average
- **Source:** Oliver Wyman (Carol Adams, Marc Lambright, Mike Kestler, John Rienstra, Kevin Salfri), *How high-cost claims push up need for stop-loss insurance*, and the companion **Fall 2026 Stop-Loss Market Update** (fourth annual), October 2026. [Page](https://www.oliverwyman.com/our-expertise/insights/2026/oct/medical-stop-loss-insurance-outlook-high-cost-claims-rise.html)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - Market-level carrier financial results for 2019–2025 plus a high-dollar claims analysis for 2020–2024.
  - The web page doesn't name the data source, carrier count or sample. Market-wide stop-loss financials normally come from statutory filings, but that is not confirmed here. I read the web summary only; the full PDF was not retrieved.
  - Oliver Wyman is a consultancy and, like Mercer, is now under the Marsh brand. Its Guy Carpenter affiliate is a reinsurance broker in this market.
- **The number:**
  - **Total-market loss ratio: 91.3% in 2025**, the highest of the six years covered.
  - **PMPM claims grew 13.1% a year (2019–2025)**, against **10.3% a year for PMPM premiums**.
  - Stop-loss premium revenue: about **$43B in 2025**, growing **12% a year** from 2020 to 2025, with covered lives roughly flat. Growth came from price, not enrollment.
  - **Incidence of claims above $2 million rose 150%**, and **above $5 million rose 267%** (2020–2024).
  - **Leveraged trend (2023–2024):** **21.3%** above a $100,000 attachment point and **44.2%** above $2 million.
  - **Inpatient services were 66.8%** of costs for people with claims over $2 million in 2024.
- **Why it matters:**
  - A 91.3% loss ratio leaves carriers almost no margin. That points to another hard renewal cycle in 2027, with higher specific premiums, lasered claimants and pressure to raise deductibles. Self-funded employers should budget for it now.
  - Leveraged trend is the key number for plan design. In 2023–2024, claims above a $100K attachment point rose 21% and above $2M rose 44%, well above overall medical trend. Holding the same deductible year after year buys less protection each year.
  - Two-thirds of the cost of the largest claims is inpatient. That is where hospital unit price, not drug cost, drives catastrophic claims. Direct contracts, case management and reference-based pricing on high-cost inpatient stays lower what reaches the stop-loss layer.
  - **Ties to unbundled positioning:** an unbundled plan can shop stop-loss separately from its TPA and network and show carriers claim-level data at renewal. A carrier-bundled ASO with an affiliated stop-loss product can't easily do either. Pair this with today's first finding: the inpatient price that drives 67% of mega-claims is the same price that anti-steering clauses protect.
  - TX-APCD angle: none directly. Stop-loss experience isn't in any APCD.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** two lines, PMPM claims (+13.1%/yr) vs PMPM premiums (+10.3%/yr), 2019–2025, captioned "Why your stop-loss renewal is going up again."
  - **Stat card:** "Claims over $5 million: +267% in four years."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "Claims above $2M are trending at 44% a year. If your specific deductible hasn't moved since 2022, your stop-loss is quietly covering less."

- **Neeraj Sood (USC Schaeffer) Senate Judiciary testimony, Nov. 17, 2026** ([PDF](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/download/2026-11-17_testimony_soodpdf)). Policy testimony, not new data, but useful for content:

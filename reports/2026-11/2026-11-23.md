### After hospitals buy physician practices, Level 5 office-visit billing rises by at least 50%, and routine imaging and labs move to higher-priced facility settings
- **Source:** Health Care Cost Institute (Jessica Chang, Catalina Desouza) and West Health (Caroline Picher, Marissa Myers, Raphael Gaeta), *After consolidation, patients directed to higher-priced sites of care*, issue brief (one-pager), September 2026; posted Sept. 29, 2026. [Page](https://healthcostinstitute.org/all-hcci-reports/after-consolidation-patients-directed-to-higher-priced-sites-of-care/) · [PDF](https://healthcostinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/WH_VI_USE_ONEPAGER_ISSUEBRIEF_20260921-1.pdf)
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - National sample of physician practices acquired by hospitals or health systems.
  - Uses HCCI commercial (employer-sponsored insurance) claims and Medicare fee-for-service claims.
  - Compares billing and referral patterns from the year before acquisition to the year after.
  - I read the one-pager only. The two full issue briefs (ESI and Medicare FFS) hold the sample size and methods; check exact figures against them before posting.
- **The number:**
  - From the year before acquisition to the year after, the share of **Level 5 E&M codes** (the highest-intensity, most expensive office visit) for established patients rose **by at least 50%**, in both employer and Medicare claims. The share of Level 1–4 codes stayed about the same or fell.
  - The share of **follow-up imaging done in facility settings**, rather than lower-cost non-facility settings, rose **13 percentage points**.
  - For **lab tests**, the facility-setting share **roughly doubled** from the year before acquisition.
  - **Context from the brief:** the share of U.S. physician practices owned by hospitals or corporate entities rose from **30% in 2018 to 64% in 2026**.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Consolidation raises employer costs on *every* visit, not only at contract renewal. The same doctor bills a higher code, and orders tests at the hospital rather than in the office or a freestanding lab. A carrier network discount does nothing about either.
  - This adds to the Nov. 18 BCBSMA finding that coding intensity drives recent spending growth. It shows one reason: ownership changes.
  - **Ties to unbundled/direct-contracting positioning:** a self-funded plan that owns its claims data can track E&M level mix and site of service by provider before and after an acquisition. It can then steer imaging and labs to freestanding sites through plan design or direct contracts. A bundled carrier ASO is paid partly on volume and has little reason to flag this.
  - **TX-APCD gap:** spotting this in Texas means linking practice ownership changes to commercial claims over time. TX-APCD excludes self-funded ERISA plans, so a Texas employer can't run this check on plans like its own using state data. **Flag as a TX-APCD data gap.**
- **Social angle:**
  - **Chart:** three bars (prior year, year of acquisition, year after) for the Level 5 share, captioned "Same doctor. New owner. 50% more top-level visits."
  - **LinkedIn take:** "When a health system buys your employees' doctor, the price is only half the story. The billing codes change, and the lab moves to the hospital. Do you know which of your providers changed hands last year?"

### 13 states now set hospital prices by reference to Medicare. Delaware is the first to cap commercial plan prices statewide, at 250% of Medicare, and Montana's state plan is heading to 180%
- **Source:** United States of Care, *State Laws to Lower Costs through Reference-Based Pricing*, policy tracker, last updated October 2026. [PDF](https://unitedstatesofcare.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/State-Successes-Passing-Laws-to-Lower-Costs-through-Reference-Based-Pricing.pdf). It's an advocacy group's compilation; each cap below comes from the state law or contract it cites. Check the statute before quoting a specific state.
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - Compiles states that have implemented provider-side or plan-side reference-based pricing by law, regulation or contract, as of October 2026.
  - Covers broad-based policies only; ambulance, surprise-billing and single-service caps are left out. States that have only studied RBP (e.g., North Carolina) are not counted.
- **The number:**
  - **13 states** have implemented reference-based pricing: CA, CO, DE, IN, MT, NV, NM, OK, OR, SC, VT, WA, WV.
  - **Delaware SB 1 (passed 2026, in effect by 2028):** caps state-regulated *commercial* plan prices at **250% of Medicare** per service. It also requires carriers to spend at least **11.5%** of medical spending on primary care.
  - **Montana state employee plan:** averaged **234% of Medicare** for hospital services (2016–2022). Its BCBS contract since 2023 caps rates at **200%, falling to 180% by 2026**.
  - **Oregon state employee and educator plans:** **200%** of Medicare in-network and **185%** out-of-network.
  - **Washington state employee plans (SB 5083, by 2027):** **200% / 185%**, with a floor of at least **150%** for primary care and behavioral health.
  - **New Mexico:** state employee plan capped at **200%** in-network and **175%** out-of-network. HB 47 (2026) extends RBP to public school employee plans.
  - **Indiana HB 1004:** large nonprofit systems must reach a reference price, likely **250–300% of Medicare**, by June 2029, or lose nonprofit status.
  - **South Carolina** state plan caps imaging, lab, ASC and physician-administered drugs at **125–135%** of Medicare.
- **Why it matters:**
  - States now pay their own employees' hospital claims at 180–234% of Medicare. RAND Round 5.1 puts the national commercial average at about 254%. Public purchasers have shown that Medicare-based caps can work across a whole state.
  - Delaware's law covers fully insured commercial plans only. ERISA preempts it for self-funded plans, so a self-funded employer gets a Medicare-based price only by contracting for one.
  - **Ties to direct-contracting/RBP positioning:** use these as benchmarks. "Your state's teachers' plan pays 200% of Medicare. What does yours pay?" is a question every self-funded employer can ask its TPA.
  - **Texas angle:** Texas is not on the list. No law, regulation or state-plan contract here benchmarks hospital prices to Medicare, so Texas employers have no state benchmark. Without self-funded claims in TX-APCD, they also can't see what comparable plans pay. **Flag as a TX-APCD data gap.**
- **Social angle:**
  - **Map:** the 13 states, shaded by cap level, with Texas blank. Caption: "13 states cap what they pay hospitals at a multiple of Medicare. Texas isn't one of them."
  - **Thread:** one post per state cap (MT 180%, OR/WA 200%, DE 250%), ending with "A self-funded plan can write its own cap into its contracts."

### Employer Net Promoter Score for PBMs fell from −10 to −25 in two years. Pharmacy is now about 36% of claims, and 84% of employers plan to take vendors to bid by 2028
- **Source:** WTW, *2026 Best Practices in Healthcare Survey*, final results press release, Oct. 28, 2026 (via [Yahoo Finance / GlobeNewswire](https://finance.yahoo.com/healthcare/articles/wtw-2026-best-practices-healthcare-140300184.html); [WTW release](https://www.wtwco.com/en-us/news/2026/10/wtw-2026-best-practices-in-healthcare-survey-us-employer-healthcare-costs)). The Aug. 26 report covered the August topline (11.1% trend, 56% unwilling to disrupt). The PBM, pharmacy share and vendor-bid figures below are new in the final release.
- **Methodology/sample:**
  - 30th annual survey; **471 U.S. employers** with 100+ employees, together employing **7 million** people.
  - Fielded June 22 – Aug. 7, 2026. Trend figures are medians.
  - The employee preference figure comes from WTW's 2026 Global Benefits Attitudes Survey (6,000 full-time U.S. employees, Feb.–May 2026).
  - Consultant survey of plan sponsor views, not claims data.
- **The number:**
  - **PBM Net Promoter Score fell from −10 (2024) to −25 (2026).** Confidence in PBM pricing, rebate program quality and coordination with medical carriers each fell by double digits.
  - **64%** of employers have taken, plan or are considering action on their PBM (rebid or switch).
  - **Pharmacy trend: 11.5% in 2026**, up from 9% in 2025. Including drugs paid under the medical benefit, pharmacy is about **36% of total claims**.
  - Leading cost pressures: high-cost claimants (**86%**) and pharmacy spending (**83%**).
  - **By 2028:** **84%** plan or are considering taking plans or vendors to bid, up from **39%** today. **63%** would lower out-of-pocket costs for high-value providers, up from **21%** today. **55%** would evaluate fraud, waste and abuse, up from **23%**. The average employer has **13 vendors**, up from 11 in 2022.
  - **By 2028**, **85%** plan or are considering raising employee premium contributions, against **44%** today.
- **Why it matters:**
  - Employers have lost faith in their PBMs, but WTW's own numbers show many are still reaching for cost-shifting (85%) before contract structure. Rebidding with the same contract terms gets you a new logo, not a transparent deal (see the Nov. 8 PSG finding).
  - Pharmacy at 36% of claims, much of it under the medical benefit, means a pharmacy carve-out that ignores medical-benefit drugs misses a large share of spend.
  - **Ties to unbundled positioning:** the jump from 21% to 63% in employers planning to steer members to high-value providers is the demand for direct contracts and tiered networks. Carrier-bundled plans struggle to deliver that.
  - TX-APCD angle: none.
- **Social angle:**
  - **Stat card:** "PBM Net Promoter Score: −10 → −25." Label it as employer sentiment, not a price finding.
  - **LinkedIn take:** "84% of employers plan to rebid vendors by 2028. Before you do, decide what you're buying: pass-through pricing, audit rights, and data you own. Otherwise it's the same contract with a new logo."

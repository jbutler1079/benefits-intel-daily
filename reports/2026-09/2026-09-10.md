### Aegis Risk's full 2026 stop-loss survey is out: employer interest in direct provider contracting almost tripled — from 4% to 11%

- **Source:** Aegis Risk, "2026 Medical Stop-Loss Premium Survey" (20th
  year); full report live on aegisrisk.com/ISCEBS, surfaced via IFEBP's
  "Word on Benefits" blog post dated September 9, 2026.
- **Methodology/sample:** 1,378 stop-loss policies, 1.4M+ covered
  employees, $1.5B+ in annual stop-loss premium.
- **The number:** 2026 premium increases ranged **13.6%** (at a $100K
  individual deductible) to **15.9%** (at $750K) versus **8.8–10.4%** at
  the same deductibles a year ago — Aegis's own illustrative 2027
  market-wide renewal projection is **14.5%**, with a warning that
  "increases exceeding 20% will not be uncommon." Among risk-mitigation
  strategies employers plan to review: **direct provider contracting rose
  from 4% (2025) to 11% (2026)** — the largest percentage-point mover in
  the survey — while reference-based pricing interest ticked from 0% to
  just 2%. Specialty-pharmacy concern jumped from 47% to 57% of
  respondents, and lasered claimants (claims specifically excluded/capped
  by the carrier) rose from 16% to 19%.
- **Why it matters:** This is the same benchmark survey that showed
  RBP/direct-contracting interest was essentially nonexistent a year ago.
  The direct-provider-contracting jump — while still a small minority of
  respondents — is the first real movement in employer interest toward
  unbundled/direct models as stop-loss costs accelerate past 15% and
  lasering becomes more common, i.e., employers running out of room with
  conventional levers.
- **Social angle:** Bar chart: risk-mitigation strategy adoption, 2025 vs
  2026, with direct provider contracting (4%→11%) as the standout mover
  against a backdrop of a 5–6pt acceleration in stop-loss premium growth.
- **Direct-contracting/RBP tie-in flag:** Direct — the survey's own
  numbers show real (if still small) employer movement toward direct
  contracting specifically.

### NAIC's "ERISA Preemption and State PBM Laws" guidance is now official — with a roadmap for states to reach self-funded plans

- **Source:** NAIC, Health Insurance and Managed Care (B) Committee
  minutes and Regulatory Framework (B) Task Force records, read directly
  from the primary documents this window (the previously-cached page had
  been serving a stale, unrelated packet).
- **Methodology/sample:** N/A — regulatory guidance document. Adopted at
  three levels: the ERISA and Alternative Health Coverage (B) Working
  Group (July 22, 2026), the Regulatory Framework (B) Task Force (Aug 13,
  2026), and the full Health Insurance and Managed Care (B) Committee
  (**Aug 14, 2026, unanimous**).
- **The number:** The guidance sets out a two-step framework analyzing
  *Rutledge v. PCMA*, *Wehbi*, and *Mulready*: (1) whether a state PBM law
  applies to self-funded ERISA plans, and (2) whether it "relates to" an
  ERISA plan under the "reference to"/"connection with" preemption test.
  Critically, it states that an **opt-in structure allowing self-funded
  plans to elect into state PBM regulation "should not, in and of itself"
  trigger ERISA preemption** — untested in court, but effectively a
  template state legislators can now use to reach self-funded plans that
  have historically relied on preemption to avoid state PBM rules. NAIC
  also recommends severability clauses in new PBM legislation.
- **Why it matters:** Self-funded employers have long treated ERISA
  preemption as a shield against state PBM regulation. NAIC just gave
  states a model for reaching self-funded plans anyway via opt-in
  structures — worth watching state legislatures for opt-in PBM bills
  built on this template, which could reshape PBM contracting
  requirements for self-funded plans on a state-by-state basis.
- **Social angle:** Explainer thread: "For years, self-funded plans could
  largely sidestep state PBM laws thanks to ERISA preemption. NAIC just
  handed states the roadmap to close that gap — here's what it says."
- **Direct-contracting/RBP tie-in flag:** Indirect — affects the PBM
  regulatory environment broadly rather than TX-APCD specifically, but
  relevant to unbundled-PBM-contracting positioning since it could expose
  self-funded plans to state pass-through/transparency mandates.
- **Bonus context:** Mark Cuban (Cost Plus Drug Company) addressed this
  same NAIC committee meeting on Aug 14 with an 8-point PBM reform
  wishlist for state regulators (cash-pay counting toward deductibles,
  standardized/public PBM contracts, rebate/fee transparency, PBM
  licensing with penalties, spread-pricing bans, limits on specialty-drug
  reclassification, anti-gag-clause enforcement). A secondary source
  suggests he repeated similar points at a Texas House hearing on Sept 1
  — see watch items below, not yet primary-source confirmed.

### MedPAC: top Medicare Advantage plans book risk-adjusted bids ~40% higher than the bottom decile — same population, different coding

- **Source:** MedPAC, Sept 3–4, 2026 public meeting deck, "MA
  risk-adjustment plan-level accuracy" (A. Johnson) — pulled and read
  directly this window after two prior sessions couldn't extract citable
  numbers from these decks.
- **Methodology/sample:** 2024 Medicare Advantage plan bids (HMO/PPO
  only, excluding Puerto Rico), risk- and geography-standardized.
- **The number:** The **90th percentile risk-standardized bid was ~40%
  higher than the 10th percentile bid** (~39% among SNPs specifically,
  similar for non-SNPs) — a gap MedPAC attributes to differences in
  risk-score coding intensity rather than underlying beneficiary health.
  Illustrative example from the deck: a plan raising its average risk
  score from 1.035 to 1.085 gains a rebate increase equivalent to a
  5-percentage-point quality-bonus benchmark bump (~$88 vs. ~$120
  illustrative rebate per enrollee).
- **Why it matters:** This is MedPAC's own commission quantifying how
  much MA plan payment variation is coding-driven rather than
  health-driven. It's Medicare Advantage-specific, not ASO/self-funded —
  the tie to employer plans is more illustrative than direct — but it's a
  hard, current number for the broader argument that opaque,
  risk/utilization-based payment models can be gamed in ways that
  transparent, direct-pay pricing can't.
- **Social angle:** Stat card: "40% bid gap. Same beneficiaries. Just
  different coding." Best used as a credibility point in a broader
  payment-opacity post rather than a standalone employer-cost piece.
- **Direct-contracting/RBP tie-in flag:** Indirect — Medicare Advantage
  specific; use as supporting evidence, not a TX-APCD or direct-employer
  data point.
- **Other MedPAC deck context (not independently reported as findings):**
  the ASC workplan deck shows 6,400 Medicare-certified ASCs in 2024 (+12%
  since 2019) and 2026/2027 OPPS rules moving hundreds of procedure codes
  onto the ASC-eligible list; the post-acute-care deck shows FFS
  beneficiaries use post-acute care at modestly higher rates than MA
  enrollees (14.2% vs. 13.2% any PAC) — both Medicare-side, noted for
  background rather than headlined.

**Correction — treat carefully, do not carry forward unverified:**

A stat that has circulated attached to Aegis Risk's stop-loss survey — "49%
of respondents report claims over $1M" — **could not be verified** in the
actual 2026 (20th-year) primary report after reading all four pages in
full. No granular high-cost-claimant frequency breakout by dollar
threshold appears in this document. That figure may belong to a different
source entirely (e.g., Sun Life's March 2026 stop-loss claims data, which
does track $1M+/$3M+ claim growth separately). Do not cite "49% >$1M" as
an Aegis Risk finding going forward without direct verification against
Sun Life's own report.

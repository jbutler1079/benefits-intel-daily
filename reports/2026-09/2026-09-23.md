### AAI's FTC comment reveals the Caremark/Zinc PBM settlement quietly released an undisclosed Aetna investigation — and its core bans only cover Caremark's negotiable "Standard Offering"

- **Source:** American Antitrust Institute, public comment filed with the
  Federal Trade Commission on the proposed Caremark Rx/Zinc Health
  Services consent order, filed **September 4, 2026** (comment period
  close date), published as "AAI Files Comments Asking FTC to Fill in
  Blanks in its Explanation of Caremark PBM Settlement in Insulin Pricing
  Case." Underlying settlement: FTC v. Caremark Rx/Zinc Health Services,
  proposed consent order published in the Federal Register **August 5,
  2026**; original FTC settlement announcement **July 14, 2026**.
- **Methodology/sample:** N/A — legal/regulatory comment analyzing the
  text of the FTC's proposed consent order and accompanying Analysis to
  Aid Public Comment, filed as part of the statutory public-comment
  process before the Commission votes to finalize.
- **The number:** The FTC's own estimate for the underlying settlement is
  up to **$8.5 billion** in "locked in" consumer savings over 10 years
  plus up to **$4.5 billion** more in "unlocked" point-of-sale rebate
  savings. AAI's comment identifies that this framing rests on prohibitions
  that reach only Caremark's **"Standard Offering"** — the order
  explicitly permits a plan sponsor and Caremark to negotiate terms that
  differ from it, the same structural flaw AAI flagged in the earlier
  Express Scripts order. AAI also reveals the consent agreement releases
  all claims from an FTC investigation of **Aetna** (CVS Health's
  insurance affiliate) that was never publicly disclosed — Aetna is not
  a respondent, is not charged in the complaint, and did not sign the
  agreement, yet its fully insured health plans carry obligations under
  the order. The Commission's underlying vote to propose the order was
  **1-0-1**, with one commissioner recused.
- **Why it matters:** This is a direct, citable challenge to how much of
  the FTC's headline PBM savings number self-funded employers should
  actually expect to see. If Caremark's real-world obligations attach
  only to a "Standard Offering" that sophisticated counterparties can
  simply negotiate away from, the settlement's structural fix may not
  reach the large, negotiated plan-sponsor contracts that matter most to
  self-funded employers — reinforcing the case that plan sponsors need
  their own contract-level protections (audit rights, pass-through terms,
  data ownership) rather than relying on federal enforcement to fix PBM
  economics for them.
- **Social angle:** "The FTC says its Caremark settlement locks in $8.5B
  in savings. A watchdog comment says the bans only cover the PBM's
  'Standard Offering' — the part sophisticated buyers can just negotiate
  around. Don't outsource your PBM contract terms to a federal consent
  order." Strong for a LinkedIn post aimed at plan sponsors relying on
  regulatory tailwinds instead of their own contract language.

### Sun Life's 2026 high-cost claims report: million-dollar-plus medical claims up 46% in four years, with gene therapy and specialty drugs leading the list

- **Source:** Sun Life U.S., annual high-cost claims report ("What
  Drives Multimillion-Dollar Medical Claims?"), published **May 21,
  2026**. **Date flag: published ~4 months before this report's window;
  included because it has not previously run as a headline item in this
  series, and it is a primary-source insurer dataset directly on stop-loss
  cost trend — a category this report otherwise had nothing fresh for
  today.**
- **Methodology/sample:** Analysis of more than **70,000** high-dollar
  medical claims from over **3,300 self-funded employer** stop-loss
  policyholders, covering claims from **2022 through 2026**.
- **The number:** Claims of **$1 million or more** increased in frequency
  by **46%** from 2022–2026. The average blood-cancer (leukemia/
  lymphoma/myeloma) claim hit **$5.45 million** in 2025, with the single
  highest leukemia claim that year near **$8 million**. The single most
  expensive drug treatment identified was **Elevidys**, a Duchenne
  muscular dystrophy gene therapy, averaging **$3.6 million** per claim.
  Liver-disease treatment spending grew **43%** in 2025 alone, averaging
  **$230,000** per patient. High-dollar claims that included GLP-1s rose
  **24%** over the past year, though per-person GLP-1 cost itself
  remained comparatively low — the claims are being driven by
  hospitalizations, gene therapies, and specialty/injectable drugs
  layered on top, not GLP-1s alone.
- **Why it matters:** This is a primary insurer dataset — not a survey of
  intentions — quantifying exactly the tail-risk pressure that is
  hardening the stop-loss market self-funded employers depend on. A 46%
  jump in seven-figure claims in four years, concentrated in a small set
  of high-cost drivers (gene therapy, blood cancer, complex hospital
  stays), is the underlying reason stop-loss renewal pricing has been
  rising well above general medical trend — a concrete number for
  conversations about captive stop-loss structures, laser exclusions, and
  why claims-level cost management (not just unit-price negotiation)
  matters for self-funded plans.
- **Social angle:** "$1M+ medical claims are up 46% in four years. The
  top driver on Sun Life's list isn't GLP-1s — it's a single gene therapy
  averaging $3.6M a claim. Stop-loss renewals are pricing this in whether
  your plan is ready or not." Strong as a stat card or as the anchor for
  a post on captive stop-loss structures.

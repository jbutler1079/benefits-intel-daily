# Daily Benefits Intelligence — 2026-09-22

Five findings cleared the bar today. The lead item is a peer-reviewed
Health Affairs Scholar study — the first to use the RAND national
benchmark as the control group — showing reference-based pricing saved
employers 56.5% vs. commercial rates. The second is the FTC's finalized

### Reference-based pricing saved employers 56.5% vs. commercial rates — $417M in one program, peer-reviewed

- **Source:** Roslyn C. Murray & Christopher M. Whaley, "Commercial prices
  relative to Medicare were lower under an employer-based reference pricing
  program," *Health Affairs Scholar*, Vol. 4, Issue 7, July 2026
  (qxag156; PubMed PMID 42441039). Data drawn from RAND Employer Hospital
  Price Transparency Study Round 5.1 (December 2024).
- **Methodology/sample:** Compared paid rates for an alternative health
  plan's reference-based pricing (RBP) program against commercial payer
  rates in the RAND PT5.1 database, using Medicare as the common benchmark.
  Universe of RAND PT5.1 covers commercial claims from hundreds of large
  self-insured employers.
- **The number:** The RBP program paid hospitals at approximately
  **140% of Medicare**. That translated to prices **118 percentage points
  lower (inpatient)** and **162 percentage points lower (outpatient)** vs.
  typical commercial payers. Employers in the program saved an estimated
  **$417 million** — equal to **56.5%** of what they would have paid at
  standard commercial rates. For context: RAND PT5.1 shows average
  commercial rates at **254% of Medicare (combined)** and **279% for
  outpatient alone**.
- **Why it matters:** This is the first peer-reviewed, RBP-vs.-commercial
  study using the RAND national benchmark as the control group — far more
  credible than vendor case studies. A 56.5% reduction in hospital facility
  costs is the difference between a sustainable self-funded plan and a
  spiraling one.
- **Social angle:** Chart post with RAND benchmark as control vs. RBP
  program rates: "Peer-reviewed, published in Health Affairs Scholar:
  employers using Medicare-anchored reference pricing paid 56.5% less than
  those on standard commercial contracts — saving $417M in one program.
  The RAND benchmark is the receipts."

### FTC finalizes CVS Caremark PBM consent order — $13B projected benefit, delinking fees from list prices required

- **Source:** FTC Proposed Consent Order — Caremark Rx LLC / Zinc Health
  Services, Federal Register, August 5, 2026 (Vol. 91, Issue 149, Doc.
  2026-15913). 30-day public comment period closed September 4, 2026.
  Parallels the Express Scripts order finalized February 2026.
- **Methodology/sample:** FTC administrative enforcement action; insulin
  and commercial formulary rebate practices affecting an estimated 80% of
  U.S. prescription drug transactions across Caremark-administered plans.
- **The number:** **$8.5 billion** in projected consumer savings from
  rebate structure reforms, plus **$4.5 billion** in patient savings from
  required point-of-sale rebate pass-through = **$13 billion total**.
  Caremark must delink PBM fees from drug list prices and manufacturer
  rebates. Low-list-price insulins must be offered competitively on
  formulary. Plan sponsors (employers) must be permitted to exit "rebate
  guarantee" and "spread pricing" models. Community pharmacies may
  transition to cost-plus reimbursement. OptumRx remains the only major
  PBM with unresolved FTC litigation.
- **Why it matters:** The order creates a federal template and precedent;
  self-funded sponsors can now point to consent order terms — delinking
  fees from list prices, mandatory rebate pass-through, plan sponsor opt-
  out from spread pricing — when renegotiating PBM contracts and insisting
  on pass-through models.
- **Social angle:** Thread: "The FTC just forced CVS Caremark to delink
  its fees from drug list prices and give employers the right to opt out
  of spread pricing. This is the contract negotiation playbook self-funded
  plans have needed. OptumRx is next. — FTC Consent Order, Fed. Register
  Aug. 5, 2026"

### CAA 2026 turns PBMs into ERISA covered service providers — $10K/day penalty for non-disclosure

- **Source:** Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2026 (CAA 2026); DOL/EBSA
  enforcement priorities guidance, September 2026. Near-primary analysis:
  Claim Informatics *Fiduciary Compliance Update*, September 2026; National
  Law Review; Frier Levitt.
- **Methodology/sample:** Federal statute and implementing guidance;
  applicable to self-funded ERISA group health plans.
- **The number:** PBMs classified as **ERISA covered service providers** —
  full compensation disclosure required before contract execution, extension,
  or renewal. **$10,000 per day** civil penalty for a PBM that fails to
  disclose. **$100,000 per item** for false or misleading disclosures.
  **100% rebate pass-through** required, remitted **quarterly**. Plan
  sponsors get **annual audit rights** using an auditor of their own
  choosing. If the PBM fails to respond within **90 days**, the plan
  fiduciary must notify DOL — or face personal liability for a prohibited
  transaction.
- **Why it matters:** CAA 2026 has converted fiduciary oversight of
  pharmacy benefits from a best practice into a legal obligation with teeth.
  Self-funded sponsors who cannot document that they reviewed, benchmarked,
  and monitored PBM compensation are now exposed to personal ERISA fiduciary
  liability — the same litigation wave that hit 401(k) plan sponsors a
  decade ago is now breaking over health plan sponsors.
- **Social angle:** Compliance-calendar alert post: "CAA 2026 turned every
  PBM into an ERISA covered service provider. Your PBM now has 90 days to
  disclose all compensation or you must notify the DOL — or face personal
  fiduciary liability. The 401(k) fee lawsuit era is coming for health
  plans."

### Pharmacy hits 25% of large-employer health spend — drug trend locked at 12% for two consecutive years

- **Source:** Business Group on Health, *2027 Employer Healthcare Strategy
  Survey*, released August 2026. Survey fielded June 2026; 127 large
  employers representing more than 11 million covered lives.
- **Methodology/sample:** Annual online survey of BGH large-employer members
  (CFO/CEO level); pharmacy trend calculated pre– and post–plan design
  change.
- **The number:** Pharmacy now accounts for **25% of total health spend**
  for large employers — up from roughly 18–19% in prior years. Drug costs
  projected to rise **12% in 2026 and 12% in 2027** (before plan design
  changes; ~10–11% after). Overall health cost trend: **9.2% median for
  2027**, reducible to ~8% with plan design changes. Cumulative cost growth
  from 2018–2027 could reach **76% — roughly double the rate of general
  inflation**. GLP-1 obesity coverage fell from **72% (2025) to 60%
  (2026)** as costs mount.
- **Why it matters:** Pharmacy has crossed a psychological and actuarial
  threshold. Self-funded plan sponsors who treat pharmacy as a secondary
  cost center are structurally misaligned with where the money is actually
  going. The GLP-1 retreat by large employers — those with the greatest
  negotiating leverage — is a leading indicator for smaller self-funded
  plans.
- **Social angle:** Trend-line chart showing pharmacy share of total spend
  (2018–2027) climbing from ~18% to 25% while drug trend holds at 12%:
  "Pharmacy just crossed 25% of total employer health spend — growing at
  12%/year for the second year running. If your plan's pharmacy strategy
  is still a 'discount off WAC' arrangement, you're funding someone else's
  rebate check."

### Three GPOs now control 84% of U.S. hospitals — up from 62% a decade ago

- **Source:** Research letter, *JAMA Health Forum*, September 2026. Authors
  analyzed GPO affiliation data for 4,760 U.S. hospitals in 2014 and 2024,
  tracking market share of Vizient, Premier, and HealthTrust.
- **Methodology/sample:** Longitudinal analysis of hospital GPO affiliation
  records; universe of U.S. acute-care hospitals; 10-year comparison.
- **The number:** In 2014, the three largest GPOs controlled **61.7%** of
  U.S. hospitals. By 2024: **84.1%** — covering **4,001 of 4,760**
  hospitals studied. Smaller GPOs' share declined from **12.3% → 10.4%**.
  Hospitals with no GPO affiliation: stable at ~**5.6%**.
- **Why it matters:** Hospital GPO dominance reduces competitive pressure
  on supply costs, reinforcing the hospital-side cost structure that
  ultimately flows into commercial prices. As employer coalitions and
  direct-contracting networks attempt to build reference pricing or bundled
  payment arrangements, concentrated supply chain incumbents that control
  hospital purchasing are a structural headwind for disruption.
- **Social angle:** Bar chart, 2014 vs. 2024 GPO share: "Three GPOs now
  control 84% of U.S. hospitals — up from 62% a decade ago. Supply chain
  concentration is the other side of hospital market power. If you're
  puzzling over why direct contracting is hard, this is part of the
  architecture. — JAMA Health Forum, Sept 2026"

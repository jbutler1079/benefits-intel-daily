### PBM-affiliated "private label" biosimilars are largely absent from their own parent companies' Medicare Part D formularies — CVS's Cordavis biosimilar got zero Part D coverage

- **Source:** Grace Castle et al. (Vanderbilt University), "Cost and
  Coverage of Originator and Biosimilar Ustekinumab Products in Medicare
  Part D," research letter, *JAMA Network Open*, published online
  **August 17, 2026** (DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.31556; PMC13483177).
  Analyzed further by Drug Channels Institute (Adam J. Fein), "The Private
  Label Biosimilar Paradox in Medicare Part D," September 1, 2026.
- **Methodology/sample:** Cross-sectional analysis of 2026 CMS formulary
  and pricing files covering 3,345 Medicare Part D plans with more than
  39 million enrollees, examining coverage of 12 ustekinumab (Stelara and
  biosimilar) products, including three "private label" biosimilars
  manufactured or marketed by companies owned by the same parent as a
  major PBM (CVS Health's Cordavis, Optum Rx's Nuvaila).
- **The number:** The average Part D plan covered only **2.3 of 12**
  available ustekinumab products. CVS Health's Cordavis-labeled biosimilar
  (Pyzchiva) received **zero** Part D plan coverage. Optum Rx's
  Nuvaila-labeled biosimilar (Wezlana) appeared on just **0.4%** of
  UnitedHealth's own Part D plans. Patients paid roughly the same
  out-of-pocket cost regardless of which product they got, so there was
  little competitive pressure pushing plans toward the cheaper option.
- **Why it matters:** PBMs have publicly defended affiliated/"private
  label" drug entities (specialty pharmacies, biosimilar labelers, GPOs)
  as pro-competitive, cost-lowering vertical integration. This study is a
  direct, peer-reviewed counterexample: when a PBM's own corporate sibling
  makes a biosimilar, the PBM's own health plans often don't even put it
  on formulary — the IRA's Part D redesign (catastrophic-phase plan
  liability jumping from 20% to 60%, new manufacturer discount
  obligations) made the economics of favoring the affiliated product
  *worse*, not better, for the vertically integrated company. That's a
  citable, non-obvious data point for employers skeptical that "PBM
  owns the pharmacy/biosimilar too" translates into savings passed
  through to the plan.
- **Social angle:** Chart/carousel — "The PBM owns the biosimilar. The
  PBM's own health plan won't cover it. Here's why." Strong for a
  LinkedIn post aimed at benefits consultants and plan sponsors
  evaluating PBM vertical integration claims.

### ERISA fee/fiduciary-breach lawsuits against health plans nearly doubled year-over-year — but fiduciary liability insurance pricing barely moved

- **Source:** Bloomberg Law, "ERISA Class Actions Soar in 2026 as New
  Legal Theories Emerge" (filing data), analyzed in actuary.info,
  "ERISA Health Plan Fee Suits: Fiduciary Liability Repricing 2026,"
  **August 2026**. Insurance pricing data from Marsh (U.S. financial and
  professional lines report, Q2 2026) and WTW's spring 2026 marketplace
  update.
- **Methodology/sample:** Tracks proposed ERISA class action filings
  against employer-sponsored health/welfare plans (fee and fiduciary-
  breach theories, including the "voluntary benefits" broker-commission
  wave) and separately tracks fiduciary liability insurance premium
  index movement over the same period.
- **The number:** Proposed ERISA class actions against health plans rose
  from **fewer than 40 in Q1 2025 to roughly 70 in Q1 2026** — filings
  have "roughly doubled year over year." Over the same period, Marsh's
  fiduciary liability composite pricing moved just **+1%**, and WTW
  described fiduciary/pension trust pricing as "flat." That's a sharp
  contrast with an earlier repricing cycle the piece cites, when a ~80%
  rise in ERISA litigation drove 15–20% fiduciary rate increases.
- **Why it matters:** This is a market-mispricing signal, not just a
  litigation-volume stat: the insurers writing fiduciary liability
  coverage for plan sponsors don't yet appear to be charging for a risk
  that has roughly doubled in a year. For a self-funded employer, that
  means fiduciary exposure from bundled, opaque broker/TPA/PBM
  arrangements is rising faster than the market is pricing it — the
  insurance safety net may reprice sharply (and suddenly) once carriers
  catch up, which is exactly the kind of hidden-liability argument that
  favors moving toward transparent, unbundled, auditable contracting now
  rather than after a rate shock.
- **Social angle:** Two-line stat post: "ERISA health-plan fee lawsuits:
  ~2x in a year. Fiduciary insurance pricing: +1%. The market hasn't
  caught up yet — you don't want to be the renewal where it does."

### Employer coverage of GLP-1s for weight loss drops from 72% to 60% in a single year — 14% say they're dropping or planning to drop it by 2027

- **Source:** Business Group on Health, 2027 Employer Healthcare Strategy
  Survey / "GLP-1 Costs Loom Large for Employers, Forcing Challenging
  Coverage Decisions" press release. Survey fielded **June 2026**;
  findings reported widely from late August into **September 8, 2026**
  coverage (Word & Brown/JRReport).
- **Methodology/sample:** Survey of **127 large employers**, representing
  more than 11 million covered lives globally (**8.7 million** in the
  U.S.).
- **The number:** Employer coverage of GLP-1s specifically for weight
  management fell from **72% in 2025 to 60% in 2026**. Another **14%** of
  employers say they're dropping coverage or planning to by 2027, up from
  an earlier-2026 estimate of 10%. **95%** of surveyed employers say
  they're concerned or very concerned about pharmacy costs, and GLP-1s'
  share of total pharmacy claims climbed from 6.9% (2023) to **11.4%**
  in 2026. Starbucks is a named example, discontinuing weight-loss GLP-1
  coverage starting October 2026.
- **Why it matters:** This is a fast reversal on a benefit that employers
  were racing to adopt 18–24 months ago, and it's happening because
  pharmacy cost trend (GLP-1s alone driving a meaningful share of the
  ~9%+ 2027 medical trend projections from Aon/WTW/Mercer/Milliman) is
  outrunning what plan sponsors are willing to absorb without utilization
  management. It's a concrete, current data point for conversations about
  formulary/PBM leverage and whether bundled PBM contracts are actually
  managing GLP-1 utilization or just passing the cost through.
- **Social angle:** Trend-line chart, 2023–2027: GLP-1 coverage rate vs.
  GLP-1 share of pharmacy spend — the two lines crossing tells the whole
  story in one image.

### DOL/EBSA's proposed rule to let health plans default to electronic ERISA disclosures — public comment period closes today, with a $3.9B/10-year savings estimate attached

- **Source:** U.S. Department of Labor, Employee Benefits Security
  Administration, "Electronic Disclosure by Group Health Plans Under
  ERISA," proposed rule, Federal Register, published **July 23, 2026**
  (RIN tracked under EBSA rulemaking). Comment period closes **today,
  September 21, 2026**.
- **Methodology/sample:** N/A (regulatory proposal); DOL's own regulatory
  impact analysis estimates apply to the roughly 2.8 million ERISA-covered
  group health plans.
- **The number:** DOL estimates the new e-disclosure safe harbor (a
  "notice-and-access" model modeled on the 2020 retirement-plan
  e-disclosure rule) would save group health plans approximately
  **$3.9 billion over 10 years** — about **$402 million per year**
  (roughly a 70% reduction in current printing/mailing spend) against an
  estimated **$30 million** first-year implementation cost. Plans
  currently print and mail up to **11 billion sheets of paper per year**
  for ERISA disclosures. Participants retain the right to request paper
  and opt out of electronic delivery entirely.
- **Why it matters:** This is a pure administrative-cost story, not a
  pricing one, but it's a real, dated compliance item self-funded plan
  sponsors and their TPAs need to track: today is the last day to weigh
  in before DOL finalizes a rule that changes the default communication
  channel for SPDs, SBCs, and other required notices. It's also a small
  but concrete illustration of how much unnecessary administrative cost
  is embedded in legacy ERISA compliance processes — relevant context
  when the same employers are being asked to accept a lack of
  transparency elsewhere in their vendor stack "because that's how it's
  always been done."
- **Social angle:** Quick-hit regulatory-calendar post: "Today's the
  deadline. DOL says its e-disclosure rule saves plans $3.9B over 10
  years — 11 billion sheets of paper a year, gone."

### NASHP's Hospital Cost Tool: the same not-for-profit hospitals that could break even on commercial payments as low as 55% of Medicare are actually being paid 334%–353% of Medicare

- **Source:** National Academy for State Health Policy (NASHP),
  "Understanding NASHP's Hospital Cost Tool: Commercial Breakeven,"
  published **September 10, 2026**, part of NASHP's Hospital Cost Tool
  (updated February 2026 with data through 2024).
- **Methodology/sample:** The Hospital Cost Tool draws on publicly
  available, hospital-attested Medicare Cost Reports for **nearly 5,000
  hospitals**, covering **2011 through 2024**. This piece walks through a
  worked example of five hospitals operating in the same city, comparing
  each hospital's "commercial breakeven" — the reimbursement rate a
  hospital needs from commercial payers to cover expenses, with no
  profit, derived from its Medicare Cost Report — against actual average
  commercial prices paid, as measured independently by the RAND
  Corporation's Hospital Price Transparency studies.
- **The number:** Across the five hospitals, commercial breakeven ranged
  from **55% to 234% of Medicare rates** (the low end being a
  government-owned facility that can cover costs below Medicare
  parity). For the not-for-profit hospitals in the group, **commercial
  breakeven sat as low as 105% of Medicare**, while RAND's independently
  measured **average commercial prices for those same hospitals ran
  334%–353% of Medicare** — a gap of more than 200 percentage points
  between what the hospital needs to stay whole and what it is actually
  collecting from commercial plans. At the health-system level, NASHP
  found median commercial breakeven of **129%–158% of Medicare** (2020–
  2022) against a RAND-measured system average price of roughly **300% of
  Medicare**, a gap that has held steady since 2020.
- **Why it matters:** This is a rare instance of two independent,
  hospital-specific datasets — one built from the hospital's own
  cost-report attestations, one from actual claims/negotiated-rate data
  — pointing at the same conclusion for named facilities: commercial
  plans are paying far more than the hospital needs to break even, not
  just more than Medicare. For self-funded employers evaluating
  reference-based pricing or direct/unbundled contracting, this is a
  hospital-by-hospital negotiating tool, not just a national statistic —
  NASHP explicitly frames breakeven as a number plan sponsors and their
  TPAs can bring into rate negotiations.
- **Social angle:** A side-by-side chart — "What this hospital needs to
  break even" vs. "What it's actually being paid" — using the 105% vs.
  353%-of-Medicare gap as the anchor stat. Strong for a LinkedIn post
  aimed at plan sponsors sizing how much room exists in a direct-
  contracting or RBP negotiation before a hospital is actually losing
  money.

### Mercer's 2027 employer survey: health benefit costs projected to rise 8.2% next year — the steepest increase since 2003 — with GLP-1s, No Surprises Act arbitration, and AI claims denials each adding about a point of trend

- **Source:** Mercer, "Survey: Health benefit costs expected to jump 8.2%
  in 2027, the biggest increase since 2003" (National Survey of
  Employer-Sponsored Health Plans, 2026), preliminary results published
  **August 31, 2026**. **Date flag: published ~3.5 weeks before this
  report's window; included because it has not previously run as a
  headline item in this series and it is the single largest primary-
  source employer-cost data point of the current cycle.** Widely
  corroborated same-week by Fierce Healthcare, The Hill, and Fortune
  (all dated September 14–23, 2026).
- **Methodology/sample:** Survey fielded starting **June 10, 2026**;
  preliminary results reflect responses from **more than 1,800
  employers** received through **August 10, 2026**; final results
  expected later in 2026 from more than 2,000 employers.
- **The number:** Total health benefit cost per employee is projected to
  rise **8.2%** on average in 2027 even after employers' planned
  cost-cutting actions — the **largest increase since 2003** and the
  **fifth consecutive year** of elevated cost growth. Without any
  mitigation, employers project costs would rise **11%**. Of that trend,
  rising **GLP-1 utilization adds roughly one full percentage point**,
  **No Surprises Act independent dispute resolution (IDR) awards add
  about one percentage point**, and **AI-assisted claims
  processing/adjudication (including increased denials) adds up to
  about one percentage point**. In response, **59% of employers plan
  cost-cutting benefit changes for 2027**, and roughly **two-thirds of
  large employers (500+ employees)** expect to shift a larger share of
  premium cost onto employees. A newer plan-design lever is gaining
  traction fast: **12% of large employers** (rising to **18% of
  employers with 20,000+ employees**) plan to offer variable/tiered
  copay plans in 2027.
- **Why it matters:** This is the actuarial-consulting data point (source
  category #11 in the research priority list) that quantifies just how
  much of next year's cost growth is coming from arbitration/dispute
  mechanisms and administrative processes rather than underlying
  utilization — the IDR and AI-claims-denial points in particular are a
  direct, citable reason plan sponsors should scrutinize their TPA's
  claims-adjudication practices and out-of-network dispute exposure, not
  just unit hospital prices, when evaluating self-funded plan
  performance.
- **Social angle:** "Health costs are set to jump the most since 2003.
  One point of that trend is GLP-1s. Another point is arbitration
  awards under the No Surprises Act. A third is AI claims denials. Only
  one of those three is really about medical care." Strong framing for a
  post distinguishing real utilization trend from administrative/dispute
  cost growth.

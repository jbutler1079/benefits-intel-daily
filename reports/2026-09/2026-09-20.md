### RAND-benchmarked study finds reference-based pricing pays hospitals 122–149% of Medicare vs. 240–311% for standard commercial plans — $417M saved across ~300,000 lives

- **Source:** Roslyn C. Murray & Christopher M. Whaley, "Commercial prices
  relative to Medicare were lower under an employer-based reference
  pricing program," *Health Affairs Scholar*, Vol. 4, Issue 7, qxag156,
  published online **June 27, 2026** (July 2026 issue). PubMed: 42441039.
  **Date flag: published ~12 weeks before this report's window. Surfaced
  now because no evidence was found that it has previously run as a
  headline item in this report series, and it is the most direct,
  quantitatively rigorous hit on the RBP/direct-contracting narrative
  found to date.**
- **Methodology/sample:** Claims-based analysis of Imagine360's
  reference-based-pricing plan — 225,618 claims / 194,392 patients
  (2024), narrowed to 188,657 inpatient + outpatient facility claims
  across 2,684 short-term acute-care/critical-access hospitals in all 50
  states + DC (Maryland excluded for its unique all-payer rate-setting
  system). Benchmarked against RAND Round 5.1 employer hospital
  price-transparency data. ~300,000 covered lives, average employer size
  650 members.
- **The number:** Inpatient facility prices = **122% of Medicare** under
  RBP vs. **240%** for standard commercial payers (a 118-point gap).
  Outpatient = **149%** vs. **311%** (a 162-point gap). Estimated total
  savings: **$417 million**, a 56.5% reduction versus standard commercial
  rates ($184M inpatient, $232M outpatient).
- **Why it matters:** This is peer-reviewed, claims-based evidence — not
  a vendor pitch deck or survey — that RBP delivers roughly half the unit
  cost of standard commercial network pricing at meaningful scale. It
  directly rebuts the "RBP causes balance-billing chaos and doesn't
  really save money" objection self-funded plan sponsors hear from
  incumbent carriers and TPAs.
- **Social angle:** "A peer-reviewed study just measured it: reference-
  based pricing plans paid hospitals 122% of Medicare for inpatient care.
  Standard commercial network plans paid 240%. That's not a rounding
  error — that's half the bill." Strong as a two-bar chart (122% vs.
  240%, 149% vs. 311%) or a savings-dollar callout ($417M / 56.5%).
- **Direct-contracting/TX-APCD tie-in flag:** Direct and strong — the
  best evidence-based anchor found this session for HPHP's core RBP/
  direct-contracting positioning; does not touch TX-APCD specifically.

### Hospital price-transparency compliance more than doubled to 49.4% — but only 18% of hospitals post real dollar-and-cents prices

- **Source:** Patient Rights Advocate (PRA), eighth semi-annual hospital
  price-transparency compliance report; reported by Medical Daily and The
  Hill, article dated **September 14, 2026**. (PRA's own report-release
  date could not be independently confirmed this session — the article
  date is contemporaneous and used as the best available anchor.) **Date
  flag: six days before this report's window; included because it does
  not appear in any prior report's carried-forward items.**
- **Methodology/sample:** Review of roughly 2,000 hospital websites and
  machine-readable files nationally.
- **The number:** **49.4%** full compliance — the highest since PRA began
  tracking in July 2021 — up from **21.1%** in PRA's prior report
  (November 2024), a 28.3-point jump. Only **18%** of hospitals post
  dollar-and-cents prices for at least half of listed items/services
  (most still use percentages, formulas, or algorithms). CMS issued 500+
  warning letters and fined 28 hospitals in its most recent enforcement
  wave, up from 15 previously.
- **Why it matters:** Compliance is improving on paper, but the 18%
  "real price" figure shows the underlying data-usability problem
  self-funded plans and APCDs still face: machine-readable files exist
  but most still aren't actionable for direct benchmarking, which is
  exactly the gap claims-based tools (RAND, HCCI, TX-APCD) and direct-
  contracting employers exist to work around.
- **Social angle:** "Hospital price-transparency compliance more than
  doubled this year — 49% vs. 21%. But only 1 in 6 hospitals post an
  actual dollar price. Compliance theater isn't the same as usable
  data."
- **Direct-contracting/TX-APCD tie-in flag:** Direct — reinforces the
  case that transparency-rule data still isn't mature enough to lean on
  alone, strengthening the argument for direct-contracting and
  claims-based benchmarking approaches (including TX-APCD, once it
  actually files — see watch items).

### JAMA: US health spending's share of GDP grew 5x slower 2009–2020 than in the two decades before it

- **Source:** Nikhil R. Sahni, Merjan L. Ozisik, David Cutler, Ezekiel J.
  Emanuel, "A Future Without Excess Health Care Spending Growth?," *JAMA*,
  published **September 14, 2026**. DOI: 10.1001/jama.2026.15970. **Date
  flag: six days before this report's window; included because it is a
  current, high-profile Perspective from leading health economists not
  previously flagged in this series (a prior report noted the piece's
  existence in passing but did not run it as a headline finding).**
- **Methodology/sample:** Literature review/analysis of national health
  expenditure share of GDP across two periods (1990–2009 vs. 2009–2020),
  identifying structural mechanisms (value-based reimbursement shifts,
  cost-saving technology adoption, ACA-era pricing and insurance-market
  provisions) behind the deceleration.
- **The number:** Health spending's GDP share grew from 17.2% to 17.7%
  between 2009 and 2020 — roughly **0.05 percentage points per year** —
  versus roughly **0.27 points per year** from 1990–2009, a **~5.4x**
  slowdown in the rate of GDP-share growth.
- **Why it matters:** Authored by Cutler and Emanuel, this reframes the
  "health spending is exploding" narrative employers hear by default. For
  HPHP's positioning, it's useful framing: RBP/direct-contracting builds
  on an already-decelerating cost trend rather than fighting an
  unstoppable one — the open question is who captures the savings.
- **Social angle:** "Health spending's share of GDP grew 5x slower from
  2009–2020 than in the two decades before it, per Cutler, Emanuel &
  Sahni in JAMA. The curve is already bending — the question for
  employers is whether they capture the savings or keep absorbing the
  leakage."
- **Direct-contracting/TX-APCD tie-in flag:** Indirect — background/
  framing value rather than a direct RBP or Texas data point.

### KFF: 2027 small-group premiums proposed up 14% at the median, as level-funded plans jump from 2% to 11%+ of the Massachusetts small-group market in four years

- **Source:** Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker, "How Much and Why
  Premiums Are Going Up for Small Businesses in 2027," published
  **August 6, 2026**. **Date flag: published ~6 weeks before this
  report's window; included because its level-funding erosion data point
  had not previously run as a headline item in this series.**
- **Methodology/sample:** Preliminary 2027 rate filings from 295
  small-group insurers across all 50 states + DC (national median);
  detailed review of 82 insurer filings across 14 states + DC. The
  level-funded enrollment figure is sourced to the Massachusetts Center
  for Health Information and Analysis (CHIA) Enrollment Trends
  Databook, via KFF's analysis of Mark Farrah Associates data.
- **The number:** 2027 median proposed increase = **14%** (25th
  percentile 10%, 75th percentile 18%; 59% of insurers proposed 10–20%
  increases), against a median underlying medical-trend estimate of
  10.8%. The fully-insured small-group market fell from ~17M covered
  lives (2013) to ~10M (2024), a 41% decline. In Massachusetts,
  level-funded small-group enrollment share rose from **2% (2021) to
  11%+ (2025)**.
- **Why it matters:** This documents the small-group "risk-pool erosion"
  mechanism directly relevant to self-funded/level-funded positioning —
  KFF's own researchers frame the shift of healthier small groups into
  level-funding as accelerating rate increases for those remaining
  fully-insured, which is the demand-side story for why self-funding and
  level-funding keep growing.
- **Social angle:** "Level-funded small-group plans went from 2% to 11%+
  of the Massachusetts market in four years. KFF's researchers call it a
  risk-pool erosion dynamic for fully-insured small business — meanwhile
  fully-insured small-group premiums are proposed up 14% for 2027."
- **Direct-contracting/TX-APCD tie-in flag:** Indirect — supports the
  broader self-funding/level-funding growth thesis rather than RBP or
  TX-APCD specifically.

### Survey: 51% of employers, 60% of health plans say they'd unbundle their PBM if procuring today; "Big 3" PBM use falls from 63% to 54%

- **Source:** Pharmaceutical Strategies Group (PSG), "2026 Trends in Drug
  Benefit Design," released **June 9, 2026**. **Date flag: published
  ~14 weeks before this report's window; included as directional context
  for unbundled-PBM positioning, but ranked last and caveated as
  self-reported intent, not realized adoption. Exact survey fielding
  dates were not stated in the available source.**
- **Methodology/sample:** Survey of 237 benefits leaders (employers,
  health plans, unions); median 14,000 covered lives per respondent
  organization.
- **The number:** **51%** of employers and **60%** of health plans said
  they'd pursue a partially or fully unbundled PBM model if conducting
  procurement today. Reported use of the "Big 3" PBMs (CVS Caremark,
  Express Scripts, Optum Rx) fell from **63% (2025) to 54% (2026)** among
  respondents, driven mostly by employers under 1,000 workers. Health-plan
  awareness of unbundled models rose to 49% reporting they'd heard "a
  lot" about it, up from 40% in 2025.
- **Why it matters:** Directly on-point for unbundled PBM positioning —
  a majority of both employers and health plans now say they'd unbundle
  if buying today — though this is stated intent from a survey, not
  confirmed switching behavior, so it should be caveated accordingly in
  any social use.
- **Social angle:** "Over half of employers now say they'd unbundle their
  PBM if they were buying today, and use of the 'Big 3' PBMs dropped from
  63% to 54% in a year. The bundled-rebate model is losing its default
  status." (Caveat: stated intent, not confirmed switching.)
- **Direct-contracting/TX-APCD tie-in flag:** Direct to unbundled-PBM
  positioning, but weakest evidentiary bar of today's five findings
  (survey rather than claims data) — ranked last for that reason.

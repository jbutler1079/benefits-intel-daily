### FDA approves Mounjaro (tirzepatide) to reduce cardiovascular events in type 2 diabetes — an 8% MACE reduction in a 13,299-patient trial

- **Source:** FDA approval announced by Eli Lilly, Aug 28, 2026, based on the SURPASS-CVOT trial. Corroborated across Lilly Investor Relations, BioSpace, Medscape, Healio, Cardiovascular Business, and BioPharm International, all dated 2026-08-28. High confidence.
- **Methodology/sample:** Phase 3, event-driven, double-blind trial; 13,299 adults with type 2 diabetes at high cardiovascular risk; 640 sites across 30 countries; tirzepatide (15mg or max tolerated dose) vs. dulaglutide (Trulicity) 1.5mg as active comparator; ~5-year trial duration, ~4-year median follow-up.
- **The number:** Tirzepatide produced an **8% lower rate of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE)** — CV death, non-fatal MI, non-fatal stroke — versus dulaglutide, itself an active GLP-1, not placebo.
- **Why it matters:** GLP-1s are already the single largest specialty-pharmacy cost pressure employers report (per this project's Aug 26 finding, 95% of employers are "concerned/very concerned" about pharmacy spend, ~25% of total plan cost). A cardiovascular-outcomes indication — not just diabetes/weight management — materially broadens the clinically-justified, likely coverage-mandated population for tirzepatide and strengthens prescriber/patient arguments against prior-authorization denials. Self-funded plan sponsors should expect this to accelerate utilization growth and sharpen the urgency of formulary, PA-criteria, and stop-loss/high-cost-claimant conversations already underway.
- **Social angle:** Stat card — "FDA just approved Mounjaro to prevent heart attacks and strokes, not just treat diabetes" — paired with a one-line translation for employers: broader indication, broader exposure. Good standalone LinkedIn post; pairs well as a follow-up to any GLP-1-cost content already in rotation.
- **Direct-contracting/RBP tie-in flag:** Indirect only — this is a PBM/formulary-management story, not a hospital-pricing/direct-contracting one. Relevant background for pharmacy-cost conversations rather than the core RBP thesis.

### EBRI: average employer HSA contribution fell to $727 in 2024 even as employee contributions and account balances hit records — cost-shifting embedded in HSA funding itself

- **Source:** EBRI, "Trends in Health Savings Account Balances, Contributions, Distributions, and Investments, 2011–2024" (Jake Spiegel and Paul Fronstin), EBRI HSA Database. **Publish-date flag:** direct WebFetch of the primary ebri.org report page returned a dateline of **Aug 13, 2026**; independent secondary coverage gives conflicting dates — 401kspecialistmag.com (Aug 19) and hsatalk.com (Aug 26) both cite the same report and figures. This is the same kind of single-report, multi-dated-mirror pattern flagged with the Business Group on Health survey on 2026-08-27/28; treating Aug 13 (direct primary fetch) as most likely correct, but citing this as a report newly surfaced by this project this window regardless of exact publish day — it has not appeared in any prior daily report. Moderate-high confidence on the figures (multiple independent corroborations agree exactly); moderate confidence on the exact publish date.
- **Methodology/sample:** EBRI HSA Database — 15.2 million HSA accounts, $53.7 billion in total assets as of Dec. 31, 2024; 23-page issue brief, 13+ years of longitudinal HSA administrative data (2011–2024).
- **The number:** Average HSA balance hit a record **$5,532** in 2024 (up from $4,747 in 2023), and average **employee** contribution rose to a record **$2,308**. Over the same year, average **employer** contribution **fell to $727** — EBRI attributes this to "a small drop in the share of employers providing a contribution" — and the report states that after adjusting for inflation, both employer and employee contributions were higher in the 2010s than they are now. Only 18% of accountholders invest HSA funds beyond cash (eighth consecutive year of increase, but still a small minority), and 56% took a distribution.
- **Why it matters:** This is a direct, dataset-level (not survey-perception) confirmation that employers are quietly reducing their share of HSA funding at the same time overall plan cost trend is accelerating (9.2% projected for 2027, per this project's Aug 26/28 finding) and employees are being asked to contribute more just to keep balances flat against rising deductibles. For a self-funded/direct-contracting pitch, this is a concrete data point that "status quo" cost management — leaning on high-deductible/HSA plan designs without addressing underlying provider pricing — is shifting cost onto employees even as employers claim to be managing spend, and gives a fiduciary-duty angle: are employers who are cutting HSA contributions while medical trend rises meeting a "reasonable" standard of employee cost-sharing support.
- **Social angle:** A two-line chart — employee contributions rising, employer contributions falling — titled "Who's actually funding the deductible gap?" Strong as a LinkedIn chart post; the $727 vs. $2,308 contrast (employer funding roughly a third of what employees now put in) is the compelling single number if space is tight.
- **Direct-contracting/RBP tie-in flag:** Indirect — supports the broader employer-cost-shifting narrative and a fiduciary-duty argument for structural (not just plan-design) cost interventions, rather than being TX-APCD/RBP-specific itself.

# Daily Benefits Intelligence — 2026-08-06

### A peer-reviewed study just measured what price transparency is actually doing to prices — and in cancer care, it's working
- **Source:** "Transparency, Repricing, and Price Convergence in Cancer Care," Value in Health (2026), researchers from Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center and Turquoise Health
- **Methodology/sample:** Analysis of payer-negotiated commercial prices for 89 oncology billing codes (inpatient, chemotherapy administration, radiation, and surgery) at 228 hospitals across the 10 largest U.S. metro areas, December 2021 through June 2024, tracking code-level price disclosure against price changes
- **The number:** Each 10-percentage-point increase in code-level price transparency was associated with a 0.82-percentage-point annual inflation-adjusted reduction in commercial oncology prices, and price convergence between high- and low-priced hospitals within the same local market ran at 21.2 percentage points per year. Critically, simply posting a machine-readable file (MRF) with no service-specific pricing showed no measurable price effect at all — the granularity is what does the work, not compliance alone.
- **Why it matters:** This is causal-design evidence, not a correlation, that *how* hospitals disclose prices determines whether transparency actually moves prices. It's directly relevant to CMS's open CY2027 RFI on standardizing MRF reporting (flagged in this project's Aug. 4 report) — this study is a data point for why format and granularity should be part of that standard, not just whether a file exists.
- **Social angle:** Line chart — price convergence trend 2021→2024 — captioned "More detail in the file. Lower price on the bill."

### 97% of U.S. hospital markets — and up to 95% of Texas metros — are legally "highly concentrated." Texas lawmakers just got the data spelled out for them.
- **Source:** Christopher M. Whaley (Brown University), testimony to the Texas House Select Committee on Health Care Affordability, April 30, 2026; Texas 2036, "Concentration, Competition and Cost: How Are the Hospital Markets of Texas?" (analysis of Health Care Cost Institute claims data)
- **Methodology/sample:** DOJ/FTC HHI-based market-concentration thresholds applied to hospital claims data nationally and across Texas's 19 major metro areas, tracked 2016 through 2024
- **The number:** 97% of U.S. metro markets for inpatient hospital care were rated "highly concentrated" in 2024 under DOJ/FTC standards. In Texas specifically, the share of the state's 19 major metros rated highly/very-highly concentrated rose from 90% (2016–2018) to 95% since 2019 — versus just 3% of California's markets, 10% of Illinois's, and 11% of Georgia's. Separately, one-third of Texas hospitals have gone through a merger or acquisition in the last decade.
- **Why it matters:** Flag prominently — direct-contracting positioning and TX-APCD data gap. This is a Texas-specific, legislatively-presented data point delivered directly to lawmakers weighing action ahead of the 2027 session: the market power driving Texas hospital prices is concentrated at levels far above comparable large states, not evenly spread. That's the structural argument for direct-contracting and reference-based pricing models that route around dominant local systems — and exactly the market-level picture TX-APCD access (opened to outside researchers this spring, per this project's Aug. 2 finding) should eventually be able to quantify at the claims level, market by market.
- **Social angle:** Flag prominently — bar chart, Texas 95% vs. CA 3% / IL 10% / GA 11%, captioned "Same country. Wildly different market power."

### System-owned hospitals charge 2.7x Medicare on average — independent hospitals charge 55+ points less, for the same care
- **Source:** Families USA, "Big Systems, Bigger Profits: National Hospital Pricing Analysis," published May 2026
- **Methodology/sample:** National analysis of hospital price transparency and negotiated-rate data, 2018–2023, broken out by hospital ownership/affiliation type (for-profit system, nonprofit system, independent/unaffiliated)
- **The number:** Commercial insurers paid hospitals an average of 2.7x what Medicare would have paid for the same services over 2018–2023. Broken out by ownership: for-profit system hospitals charged 297% of Medicare, nonprofit system hospitals charged nearly as much at 276%, while independent (non-system) hospitals charged 221% of Medicare — 55 to 76 percentage points lower than their system-owned peers for identical care.
- **Why it matters:** Flag prominently — direct-contracting positioning. This is national, multi-year confirmation that hospital system affiliation itself — independent of nonprofit-vs-for-profit status — is the single biggest lever on commercial price. It's the data-backed case for employers to steer volume toward independent hospitals and ASCs specifically, not just toward generically "lower-cost" providers.
- **Social angle:** Flag prominently — 3-bar chart: independent (221%) vs. nonprofit system (276%) vs. for-profit system (297%), captioned "It's not who owns the hospital. It's whether it's part of a system."

### The FTC just forced the country's largest PBM to stop hiding behind rebates — and a parallel DOL rule would let self-funded plans demand the same disclosure
- **Source:** FTC v. Express Scripts, Inc. settlement, announced Feb. 4, 2026; U.S. Department of Labor proposed rule on PBM compensation disclosure for ERISA self-funded plans, published Jan. 30, 2026
- **Methodology/sample:** Not a data study — an FTC consent settlement (from its insulin-pricing/formulary-placement enforcement action) and a DOL notice-and-comment rulemaking under CAA 2026 transparency provisions
- **The number:** Express Scripts must delink its compensation from the size of the rebates it negotiates with manufacturers, stop preferring high-list-price drugs on its standard formulary when cheaper clinical equivalents exist, and move to a cost-plus pricing model by 2028 — under 10 years of FTC monitoring. The FTC estimates the changes will generate up to $7 billion in patient out-of-pocket savings over that period. Separately, DOL's Jan. 30 proposed rule would require PBMs serving self-funded ERISA plans to disclose direct and indirect compensation — manufacturer rebates, spread pricing, pharmacy clawbacks — and would give plan sponsors explicit rights to audit PBM records.
- **Why it matters:** Flag prominently — unbundled/carve-out PBM positioning. Together, these give self-funded plan sponsors both a live enforcement precedent (rebate-linked PBM compensation is now legally disfavored at the largest player in the market) and, pending finalization, a regulatory lever (mandatory audit rights) to demand the same transparency from their own PBM contracts — without waiting on a full carve-out to get there.
- **Social angle:** Two-panel graphic — "FTC settlement: no more rebate-linked pay" / "DOL proposed rule: your plan gets audit rights" — captioned "The rebate model is losing its legal cover."

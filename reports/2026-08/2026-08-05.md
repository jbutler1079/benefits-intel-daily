# Daily Benefits Intelligence — 2026-08-05

### At the $1M deductible level, lymphoid leukemia claims outnumber breast cancer claims more than 12 to 1 — the cancer mix stop-loss carriers see flips entirely as claims get catastrophic
- **Source:** QBE North America, "2026 Accident & Health Market Report," published June 2026 (coverage: PR Newswire, Insurance Business, Risk & Insurance, Business Insurance)
- **Methodology/sample:** QBE's own book of employer stop-loss claims, analyzed across deductible bands ($100K up through $1M+) with multi-year frequency/severity trending
- **The number:** Neoplasms remain the single largest diagnosis category in stop-loss reimbursements at 36% of all dollars, the top driver at every deductible level. At the $200K deductible, neoplasm severity is up 12% and frequency up nearly 30% versus the prior-year average, and claims exceeding $1M have nearly tripled in frequency over five years (with $500K/$750K/$1M/$2M threshold claims all rising 2024→2025). But the composition of "cancer" flips by claim size: breast cancer is 17% of cancer claims at the $100K deductible but only 3% at $1M, while lymphoid leukemia is just 3% at $100K but 38% at $1M — driven by high-cost specialty and CAR-T therapies.
- **Why it matters:** Employers and underwriters pricing off "cancer is ~36% of claims" as a flat number are missing that the disease mix driving catastrophic ($1M+) claims is not more breast cancer — it's a small number of blood-cancer cases on six- and seven-figure specialty-drug regimens. That's a distinct laddered-stop-loss and specialty-pharmacy conversation, not a general oncology cost-containment one.
- **Social angle:** Flag prominently — stop-loss trend data. Paired stacked-bar chart: cancer-type mix at $100K deductible vs. $1M deductible, captioned "Same disease category. Completely different cancer once claims go catastrophic."

### A Texas public university employee a few hundred miles away pays $7,600+ more for individual coverage — for the same job, same state, same employer type
- **Source:** Rice University Baker Institute for Public Policy, "Texas Universities Show Sharp Differences in Health Insurance Premiums" (Vivian Ho et al.), published May 20, 2026
- **Methodology/sample:** Comparison of 2025–26 plan-year premiums and actuarial values across Texas colleges/universities enrolling 1,000+ students
- **The number:** Total annual individual-coverage cost (employer + employee contribution combined) ranges from $8,095 at ERS-system institutions (e.g., University of Houston) to $15,742 at Trinity University — a $7,647 spread. Family coverage varies by more than $25,000 across the sample. Plan richness (actuarial value) ranges from 0.815 (SMU) to 0.88 (Rice's POS plan), a 6.5-point gap worth roughly $400/year in added out-of-pocket cost for an average-spending employee. Separately: Blue Cross Blue Shield of Texas controls roughly 60% of the state's PPO market and negotiates the rates behind the two lowest-cost plans in the sample (ERS and the UT System).
- **Why it matters:** This is a controlled comparison — same state, same public-sector employer category, similar labor markets — and it still shows five-figure premium swings tied to which network/negotiator an institution uses. It's a clean, Texas-specific illustration of the carrier-negotiation-drives-price effect that direct-contracting pitches are built on. It's also exactly the kind of variation TX-APCD access (opened to outside researchers only this past March, per this project's Aug. 2 finding) should be able to show at statewide scale — single-sector studies like this one are filling the gap in the meantime, not replacing the need for it.
- **Social angle:** Flag prominently — direct-contracting positioning and TX-APCD data gap. Range-bar chart across the ~30 Texas institutions studied, captioned "Same state. Same employer type. $7,600 apart."

### A stop-loss carrier raised prices 24% for 2026 — while its own loss ratio improved to 87%, down from 90% the year before
- **Source:** Voya Financial, Q1 2026 earnings disclosures (reported May 6–7, 2026) and "Stop Loss Insurance Paid Claims Analysis" (2026 edition), as covered by HR Executive (Feb. 13, 2026) and BenefitsPro (Feb. 4 and May 7, 2026)
- **Methodology/sample:** Voya's own employer stop-loss book; loss ratio = stop-loss claims paid ÷ stop-loss premium revenue, disclosed quarterly; claims incidence tracked separately in Voya's annual paid-claims analysis
- **The number:** Voya raised stop-loss prices an average of 24% for Jan. 1, 2026 renewals. Despite that, Q1 2026 stop-loss sales still grew 5.7% to $280M, and the loss ratio — the share of premium paid back out in claims — fell to 87%, down from 90% in 2025 and 89% in 2024. Separately, Voya's claims incidence rate climbed from 23.8 to 32.5 (a 37% jump), with cancer the single largest driver of both claim count and cost.
- **Why it matters:** Claims frequency is genuinely rising — that 37% incidence jump is real cost pressure. But the pricing response is outrunning even that: a falling loss ratio alongside a 24% rate hike means Voya is keeping a larger share of every premium dollar as margin, not just passing through worse claims experience. For self-funded employers, that's direct evidence that stop-loss renewal increases have a margin-expansion component layered on top of trend — the core case for evaluating captives, laddered/aggregating stop-loss, or retained risk instead of accepting renewal terms as a pure pass-through.
- **Social angle:** Two-line chart — rate increase (24%) vs. loss ratio trend (89% → 90% → 87%) — captioned "The carrier is pricing ahead of its own numbers."

### Commercial insurers pay $1,489 more per procedure at a hospital outpatient department than at an ambulatory surgery center — for the identical service, from the same three insurers
- **Source:** Health Affairs 44(10), "Commercial Insurers Paid More For Procedures At Hospital Outpatient Departments Than At Ambulatory Surgical Centers," Maughan, Ryan, Radhakrishnan & Whaley (Brown University), October 2025
- **Methodology/sample:** 2024 Transparency in Coverage negotiated-rate data from three national insurers (UnitedHealthcare, Cigna, BlueCross BlueShield), compared across thirteen common outpatient procedures performed at both HOPDs and ASCs
- **The number:** Commercial prices were $1,489 (78%) higher at hospital outpatient departments than at ambulatory surgery centers for the same procedures in 2024 — a larger dollar gap than Medicare's own HOPD/ASC differential of $633 (97%), because the commercial ASC baseline itself already sits well above what Medicare pays for the identical service.
- **Why it matters:** This is peer-reviewed, apples-to-apples confirmation — thirteen identical procedures, three major national carriers — that the HOPD/ASC price gap isn't a policy-model assumption, it's what insurers are paying today. It directly quantifies the savings available to any self-funded employer or TPA willing to steer non-emergency outpatient procedures toward ASCs, independent of which carrier network the employer uses.
- **Social angle:** Flag prominently — direct-contracting/site-of-care steerage. Bar chart for 2–3 flagship procedures (e.g., colonoscopy, knee arthroscopy) comparing HOPD vs. ASC price, captioned "$1,489. Same procedure. Different building."

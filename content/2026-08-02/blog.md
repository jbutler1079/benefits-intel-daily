## The 2026 cost curve is structural — and that changes what self-funded employers should do about it

Every renewal season produces a fresh batch of scary percentages. What makes the 2026 data different isn't the size of any single number — it's how many independent sources are pointing the same direction, and how clearly they separate the employers who tweak from the employers who restructure.

Here's what this week's research says, and why it argues for pulling structural levers rather than trimming plan design at the margins.

### Your reinsurance bill is supposed to track claims. It doesn't.

Start with stop-loss, because it's the line item most employers assume moves in lockstep with their claims. It doesn't. The 2025 Aegis Risk / IFEBP Medical Stop-Loss Premium Survey — covering 1,268 self-funded plan sponsors and more than $1.2 billion in specific stop-loss premium — puts 2025–2026 renewal increases at 8.8% (at a $100,000 specific deductible) to 10.1% (at $500,000). The longer-term premium trend runs 9.9%–12.1%, comfortably above underlying medical trend of roughly 7–8%.

That gap is structural, not a one-year anomaly. A stop-loss carrier only pays claims above your deductible, so a modest rise in total claims translates into a much larger percentage rise in the reinsurer's exposure — and premium. It's the "leveraged trend" effect, and it compounds every renewal cycle. Any employer benchmarking their reinsurance renewal against headline medical trend is quietly underestimating their real cost curve.

### Three surveys, one signal

Zoom out from stop-loss to total medical trend, and the "this is structural" theme repeats. Three surveys — run by three different organizations, using three different methodologies — converge on near-double-digit, multi-year growth:

- **Mercer** projects +6.5% for 2026, the highest since 2010 — but notes employers themselves estimate +9% if they take no cost-management action.
- **Aon** projects +9.5%, pushing average cost past $17,000 per employee.
- **WTW** projects +9.2%, the fastest pace since at least 2011.

One firm's forecast is an opinion. An employer survey, a set of aggregated carrier trend filings, and a global insurer survey all landing in the same range is a signal. And a multi-year signal undercuts the comforting story that 2025–2026 was a one-time spike you can wait out.

### Where the structural savings actually live: same building, same code

If trend is structural, the response has to be too — and the clearest evidence base for a structural response is hospital price transparency. Turquoise Health's repricing analysis of CMS machine-readable files looked at 500 common services using post-2026-rule data, which now requires actual dollar amounts (not estimates) and facility NPIs that let datasets be linked. The finding: self-insured employers who shift claims from their highest-rate carrier/plan combination to the lowest-rate one **at the same hospital** save an average of 27%. Reference-based pricing deployments built on the same transparency data have documented 15%–30% savings.

The important word is *same*. This isn't narrowing the network or steering patients to a different facility. It's evidence that carrier selection alone — at the identical building, for the identical procedure code — creates double-digit price swings. That's the core case for unbundling the carrier from the network and pricing directly off transparency data, which is exactly where a direct-contracting model earns its keep.

### The lever hospital repricing won't pull

No honest cost story is complete without pharmacy. The IFEBP GLP-1 2026 Pulse Survey (roughly 300 U.S. employer plans, fielded June 2026) reports that GLP-1 drugs now account for 11.4% of annual claims dollars, up from 6.9% in 2023 — a 65% relative increase in three years. Yet only 36% of employers cover GLP-1s for both diabetes and weight management, barely up from 34% in 2024.

Read those two numbers together: claims share is rising faster than coverage generosity. The pressure is coming from utilization and price, not benefit design. That means facility-price fixes alone won't contain it. Self-funded employers evaluating direct contracting or reference-based pricing need a pharmacy-specific lever running in parallel — manufacturer-direct channels, carve-outs — rather than assuming hospital repricing will carry the whole load.

### And if you're citing Texas data, know what you're standing on

One caution for anyone building a Texas-specific case. The Texas All-Payor Claims Database (TX-APCD), housed at the UTHealth Houston Center for Health Care Data and established under HB 2090 in September 2021, pulls from 120-plus payors covering roughly 60% of Texans with medical coverage — 80% including pharmacy and dental. But qualified research entity applications only opened in January 2026, with approved researchers gaining actual data access in March 2026. That's about 4.5 years of statewide claims data accumulating before any outside researcher could query it.

The practical implication: much of the "Texas cost variation" data floating around has effectively come from a black box until this spring. A credible TX-focused direct-contracting pitch should name that gap explicitly — and lean on the newly-available primary data — rather than gloss over where its numbers come from.

### The through-line

Stop-loss leverage, converging trend surveys, same-facility price swings, GLP-1 claims share, and a just-opened Texas dataset don't look related on the surface. They tell one story: 2026 cost growth is structural, so the responses that move the needle are structural too — unbundling the carrier from the network, adding a distinct pharmacy strategy, and grounding regional claims in primary data rather than vendor assertion.
